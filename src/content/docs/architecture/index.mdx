## Overview

**Software architecture is the set of decisions that are expensive to change
later.** Not every design choice is architectural — the name of a variable, the
shape of a function, the framework you pick for a leaf component are all cheap to
revise once you learn more. Architecture is the small subset of decisions that
everything else depends on: how the system is decomposed into parts, how those
parts communicate, where state lives, what is allowed to fail independently, and
which boundaries you are willing to defend. Get these wrong and you pay interest
on them for years; the cost of reversal grows as the codebase, the team, and the
data accrete on top of the original choice.

The trap is treating architecture as a phase ("we did the architecture") or a
diagram (the boxes-and-arrows poster nobody reads). It is neither. It is an
ongoing stream of *significant* decisions, each of which should be made
deliberately, recorded with its rationale, and revisited when the constraints
that justified it change. This cluster is about that discipline at the
**system and service level** — how to slice a system into services, where the
boundaries come from, how the pieces communicate, and how to decide and record
all of it. For *code-structure* patterns (Hexagonal, CQRS, Event Sourcing, Saga
as implementation shapes) see the
[Architectural patterns](/design-patterns/architectural-patterns/) page in the
Design Patterns cluster; here we treat those concerns one level up, as choices
about whole systems.

## Mental model

Hold three ideas together and most architecture decisions become legible.

**1. Architecture is the high-cost-of-change set.** Ralph Johnson's definition —
"architecture is the things that are hard to change" — and "the decisions you
wish you could get right early." The test for whether a decision is
architectural is simple: *how much would it cost, in time and risk, to reverse it
a year from now with the system in production?* High cost means architectural,
treat it with care. Low cost means it is a detail; defer it and stay flexible.

**2. Architecture serves quality attributes, not features.** Functional
requirements ("the user can reset their password") can usually be satisfied by
almost any architecture. What separates a good architecture from a bad one is how
well it delivers the **quality attributes** — the "-ilities": scalability,
availability, latency, security, modifiability, testability, observability,
deployability, cost-efficiency. These are the real drivers. An architecture is
"right" only relative to the quality attributes that actually matter for this
system, ranked and quantified.

**3. Architecture is the art of trading off.** There is no best architecture,
only the least-wrong architecture for a given set of prioritized constraints.
Every "-ility" you optimize, you pay for in another. Strong consistency costs
availability under partition. Low latency costs cost. High modifiability (many
small services) costs operational complexity and end-to-end latency. The
architect's job is not to maximize one attribute but to find the point on the
tradeoff surface that the business actually wants — and to make that point
explicit so the team isn't silently optimizing for the wrong thing.

## Types / Variants

The raw materials of architectural thinking — the concepts you reason *with*.

**Quality attributes (the -ilities).** The non-functional properties an
architecture must deliver. They split roughly into *runtime* attributes
(performance/latency, availability, scalability, security, resilience) and
*development-time* attributes (modifiability, testability, deployability,
buildability). They are in constant tension; you cannot maximize all of them, so
they must be ranked. A useful sharpening tool is the **quality-attribute
scenario**: "When [stimulus] occurs under [conditions], the system shall
[response] within [measure]" — e.g. "When traffic spikes 10x within one minute,
the system serves p99 under 300ms by autoscaling within two minutes." Vague -ilities
("it should be scalable") are useless; scenarios make them testable.

**Architectural drivers.** The inputs that *force* architectural decisions.
Four kinds: (1) **prioritized quality attributes**; (2) **key functional
requirements** — the handful of features that shape structure (a real-time bid
engine, a regulatory ledger); (3) **constraints** you cannot change (must run on
-prem, must use the existing Oracle DB, RBI data-residency rules, a fixed team);
and (4) **architectural concerns** — cross-cutting things you must address
(auth, logging, deployment) regardless of features. Drivers are the *why* behind
every decision; if a decision can't be traced to a driver, question it.

**Fitness functions.** Automated, objective checks that the architecture still
holds the properties you care about — borrowed from *evolutionary architecture*
(Ford, Parsons, Kua). Examples: a test that fails the build if the domain layer
imports an infrastructure package (guards the dependency rule); a load test that
fails if p99 latency regresses past a threshold; a cycle-detection check on the
module dependency graph; a license/CVE scan. Fitness functions turn "the
architecture says X" from a wish into an *enforced invariant* that survives team
turnover and refactors.

**Conway's law.** "Organizations design systems that mirror their own
communication structure." This is not a slogan — it is a constraint you build
*with* or fight *against*. If three teams build a system, you will get
(approximately) three components, with the integration seams at the team
boundaries. The **inverse Conway maneuver** uses this deliberately: design the
team topology you want the architecture to have, and the architecture tends to
follow. Microservice boundaries that cut across team boundaries fail; the
org chart wins.

**The cost of wrong abstractions.** "Duplication is far cheaper than the wrong
abstraction" (Sandi Metz). A premature or wrong abstraction — a shared library,
a generic "platform," a too-early microservice boundary — couples things that
should have stayed independent, and is far harder to unwind than to have written
the duplication and abstracted later once the real shape was visible. Wrong
abstractions are a leading cause of accidental complexity; the architectural
discipline is to defer abstraction until the pattern is genuinely clear (the
**rule of three**) and to prefer a wrong-but-cheap-to-fix decision over a
right-looking-but-expensive-to-reverse one.

**Architecture as tradeoffs.** The meta-variant: every architectural style,
pattern, and decision is a position on one or more tradeoff axes. The skill is
not knowing the patterns (anyone can list them) but knowing *what each one
costs* and matching that cost profile to your prioritized drivers.

## When to use / When NOT

This page is about the *discipline* of architecting, so "use when" is really
"how much architecture is warranted."

- **Invest heavily in upfront architecture when:** the cost of getting it wrong
  is high and the constraints are well understood — regulated domains (lending,
  payments, healthcare), systems with hard scale or latency SLAs from day one,
  platforms many teams will build on, or anything where a structural mistake is
  expensive to unwind. Here, deliberate decisions and recorded rationale pay off.
- **Keep architecture deliberately thin and defer decisions when:** you are
  early, the problem is poorly understood, or you are exploring product-market
  fit. Premature architecture (microservices on day one, a generic platform
  before the second use case) is a classic, costly mistake — you pay the
  complexity now and frequently abstract the *wrong* thing. Start with the
  simplest structure that could work (often a modular monolith) and let real
  drivers pull complexity in.
- **Always:** identify and rank quality attributes, trace decisions to drivers,
  and record the significant ones. This is cheap and pays off at any scale.
- **Avoid:** optimizing -ilities nobody asked for (gold-plating for a scale that
  will never arrive), treating the architecture diagram as the deliverable, or
  making structural decisions implicitly (where state lives, what can fail
  independently) and discovering them only when they break.

## Tradeoffs

Architecture is *defined* by tradeoffs. The recurring tensions:

| Axis | Optimizing the left costs you | Optimizing the right costs you |
| ---- | ----------------------------- | ------------------------------ |
| Simplicity ↔ Flexibility | Future change is harder/coupled | Accidental complexity now, YAGNI risk |
| Consistency ↔ Availability | Reduced availability under partition | Stale/conflicting reads, reconciliation |
| Performance ↔ Modifiability | Coupled, hard-to-change code | Indirection, latency overhead |
| Few services ↔ Many services | Coupled deploys, scaling whole units | Network/ops complexity, distributed debugging |
| Build ↔ Buy | Time, maintenance, opportunity cost | Vendor lock-in, fit gaps, less control |
| Decide now ↔ Decide later | Risk of wrong-and-expensive choice | Uncertainty, rework if deferred too long |

The discipline is to make these tradeoffs **explicit and prioritized** rather
than stumbling into them. Two systems with identical features can sit at opposite
ends of every axis above and both be correct — because their drivers differ.

## Real-world examples

- **Amazon's "two-pizza teams"** are a deliberate inverse-Conway move: small,
  autonomous teams each owning a service, so the org structure produces the
  service-oriented architecture Amazon wanted. The architecture mirrors the
  teams by design, not by accident.
- **Evolutionary architecture / fitness functions** are practiced at
  ThoughtWorks and many platform teams via tools like ArchUnit (JVM) and
  dependency-cruiser (JS), which fail the build on layering violations or
  dependency cycles — making the dependency rule an enforced invariant.
- **The "wrong abstraction" tax** shows up everywhere a shared internal library
  or premature microservice had to be torn back out; many teams (famously
  Segment, who *reverted* microservices back to a monolith) found the
  abstraction cost exceeded its benefit and consolidated.
- **Regulated fintech** (lending, payments) is a canonical "invest upfront"
  case: data-residency, auditability, and consistency constraints are
  architectural drivers that must shape structure from day one, because retro
  fitting them is prohibitively expensive.
