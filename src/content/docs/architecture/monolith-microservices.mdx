## Overview

"Monolith versus microservices" is the most over-debated and most misunderstood
decision in modern architecture. It is usually framed as a binary — old-and-bad
monolith versus new-and-good microservices — when it is really a **spectrum** of
deployment granularity, and the right point on that spectrum depends entirely on
your drivers: team size, scaling needs, rate of change, and operational maturity.
The genuinely important variable is not "how many deployable units" but
**coupling and cohesion**: whether the things that change together live together,
and whether the things that should be independent actually are.

The most expensive mistake in this space is jumping straight to fine-grained
microservices to chase a benefit (team autonomy, independent scaling) before you
have the operational platform, the clear domain boundaries, or the organizational
need that makes them pay off — and ending up with a **distributed monolith**:
all the complexity of a distributed system with none of the independence. The
modern, hard-won default for most systems is to start with a **modular
monolith** and extract services only where a concrete driver demands it.

## Mental model

Picture a single dial labeled **deployment granularity**, and a separate, more
important dial labeled **coupling**.

- The granularity dial runs: *single deployable* → *a few coarse services* →
  *many fine-grained services*. Turning it right buys independence
  (deploy, scale, fail in isolation) and costs network hops, operational
  surface, and distributed-systems complexity.
- The coupling dial is what actually determines pain. You can have a
  well-modularized monolith (low coupling, one deployable) or a badly-coupled
  microservices mess (high coupling, many deployables — the distributed
  monolith). **Microservices do not reduce coupling; they make coupling
  expensive**, which forces you to confront it, but only if the boundaries are
  drawn right.

The decision is therefore: *turn the granularity dial right only as far as a
driver pushes you, and never faster than you can keep the coupling dial low.*
The killer test for a service boundary: **can this service be developed,
deployed, and scaled independently of the others, without a chain of coordinated
releases?** If a "service" must be released in lockstep with three others, it is
not a service — it is a distributed module, and you are paying network cost for a
function call.

## Types / Variants

**Monolith (single-process).** One deployable unit, one codebase, one database
(usually), one process. *Strengths:* simplest to build, test, deploy, and debug;
in-process calls are fast and transactional; no network failure modes; easy to
refactor across boundaries. *Weaknesses:* as it and the team grow, modules tend to
couple, the build/test/deploy cycle slows, and you must scale and deploy the whole
thing together. A poorly-structured monolith becomes a **big ball of mud**. Note:
"monolith" is not an insult — a clean monolith is the right answer for a large
share of systems.

**Modular monolith.** A single deployable, but with *strictly enforced internal
module boundaries* — modules communicate through explicit interfaces, own their
data (no reaching into another module's tables), and ideally have dependency
rules enforced by fitness functions. *Strengths:* most of the modifiability and
team-ownership benefits of services, with in-process speed, transactional
simplicity, and one thing to operate. *Why it matters:* it keeps the *option* to
extract a module into a service cheap, because the seam already exists. This is
the recommended starting point for most non-trivial systems.

**Service-Oriented Architecture (SOA).** The 2000s-era predecessor:
coarse-grained services exposed over an enterprise service bus (ESB), often with
shared databases and heavyweight protocols (SOAP/WS-*). *Intent:* reuse and
integration across a large enterprise. *Weakness:* the ESB became a centralized,
smart bottleneck and services frequently shared data, re-coupling them.
Microservices are partly a reaction to SOA: "smart endpoints, dumb pipes,"
decentralized data, lighter protocols.

**Microservices.** Many small, independently deployable services, each owning its
data store (**database-per-service**), each ideally aligned to a business
capability / bounded context, communicating over the network (REST, gRPC, or
async events). *Strengths:* independent deployment, independent scaling per
service, fault isolation, technology heterogeneity, and — crucially — *team
autonomy* at scale. *Weaknesses:* distributed-systems complexity (partial
failure, network latency, eventual consistency, no distributed transactions),
heavy operational and observability requirements, and a steep platform cost.
Pays off mainly at organizational scale.

**The spectrum (and the trap).** Between these sit coarse-grained services,
"macroservices," and self-contained systems. The anti-pattern that lurks across
the whole right half is the **distributed monolith**: services that must be
deployed together, share a database, or call each other synchronously in long
chains — you pay every microservices cost and earn none of the independence.

## When to use / When NOT

- **Monolith / modular monolith — use when:** the team is small-to-medium (up to
  ~a few teams), the domain boundaries are still being discovered, you want speed
  of development and simple operations, and you have no concrete need to scale or
  deploy parts independently. **This is the correct default.** **Avoid when:**
  you have genuinely independent scaling needs you cannot meet by scaling the
  whole unit, or many teams are blocked on a shared deploy pipeline.
- **Microservices — use when:** multiple teams need to deploy independently
  without coordinating (the dominant real driver), specific components have
  *very* different scaling or availability profiles, you need fault isolation
  between subsystems, AND you already have the operational platform (CI/CD,
  observability, infra automation, on-call maturity) to run a distributed system.
  **Avoid when:** you are a small team, the domain boundaries are unclear,
  you lack the operational platform, or you are adopting them for résumé/fashion
  reasons. Microservices solve an *organizational-scaling* problem first and a
  technical-scaling problem second.
- **SOA / ESB — use when:** rarely, in legacy enterprise-integration contexts.
  For new systems prefer microservices-style decentralization or a modular
  monolith. **Avoid:** building a new smart-pipe ESB as a coupling point.

## Tradeoffs

| Concern | Monolith | Modular monolith | Microservices |
| ------- | -------- | ---------------- | ------------- |
| Deployment | One unit, all-at-once | One unit, clean seams | Independent per service |
| Scaling | Whole app together | Whole app together | Per service, granular |
| Dev speed (small team) | Fast | Fast | Slowed by infra overhead |
| Dev speed (many teams) | Coordination bottleneck | Better, still one pipeline | Independent, high |
| Operational complexity | Low | Low | High (platform required) |
| Failure mode | Process-wide | Process-wide | Isolated (if done right) |
| Consistency | Strong, transactional | Strong, transactional | Eventual, sagas |
| Refactoring boundaries | Cheap (in-process) | Cheap (in-process) | Expensive (network contract) |
| Worst case | Big ball of mud | Coupled modules | Distributed monolith |

Cohesion-and-coupling cuts across all of it: the decision is less "mono vs micro"
and more "are my boundaries drawn where the system actually changes." A modular
monolith with good boundaries beats microservices with bad ones every time.

## Real-world examples

- **Shopify** runs one of the largest **modular monoliths** in the world (Ruby on
  Rails), with enforced internal component boundaries — a deliberate choice to
  keep development velocity high without paying the microservices tax.
- **Amazon and Netflix** are the canonical microservices adopters, driven first
  by *organizational* scale (thousands of engineers deploying independently) and
  backed by enormous investment in deployment and observability platforms.
- **Segment** famously *reverted* from microservices back toward a monolith after
  the operational and dependency-management overhead of ~140 services exceeded
  the benefit — a widely-cited cautionary tale about premature decomposition.
- **Uber** moved from monolith to thousands of microservices, then introduced
  "domain-oriented microservice architecture" (coarser, capability-aligned
  groupings) to tame the fragmentation — a real example of the spectrum being a
  spectrum, not a binary.
