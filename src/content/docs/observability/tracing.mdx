## Overview

**Distributed tracing** follows a single request as it fans out across services, recording the
causal, timed path it took. Where a metric says "p99 latency is 2s" and a log says "this one call
failed," a trace answers the question neither can alone: *which of the eleven services on this
request was the slow one, and what was it waiting on?* In a monolith you'd attach a profiler; in a
microservice system the request crosses process and network boundaries, so the "stack trace" must
be reconstructed from pieces emitted by each service and stitched together by a shared identifier.
That stitched structure — a tree of timed **spans** under one **trace ID** — is what makes latency
in a distributed system debuggable. Tracing is the most powerful pillar for cross-service latency
attribution and also the most expensive at full fidelity, so the central operational question is
**how to sample** — keeping the interesting traces without keeping (and paying for) all of them.

## Mental model

**A trace is a distributed stack trace with timing; a span is one stack frame.** A **span** is a
single unit of work — one service handling one operation — with a start time, a duration, a name,
a set of **attributes** (key-value metadata: `http.method`, `db.statement`, `user.tier`), and a
**parent span ID**. The first span (the edge request) is the **root**; every downstream call
creates a **child** span pointing back at its parent. Assemble all spans sharing a **trace ID** by
their parent links and you get a tree — render it on a time axis and you get a **waterfall**: each
span a horizontal bar, nesting showing causality, length showing duration. Reading the waterfall,
the slow hop is the long bar; a serial chain of bars is sequential dependency; overlapping bars are
concurrency. The whole point is that latency *attribution* — "the 2 seconds were spent here" —
becomes visual and obvious.

Second model: **the magic is context propagation, and it must be unbroken.** A trace only stitches
together if every service forwards the trace context (the `trace_id` plus the current `span_id`,
carried in a standard header — **W3C Trace Context**'s `traceparent`) on every outbound call, and
each receiver makes its new span a child of the incoming one. Break the chain *anywhere* — a
service that drops the header, an async queue that doesn't carry it, a thread boundary that loses
it — and the trace splits into disconnected fragments, hiding exactly the cross-boundary latency you
were chasing. Propagation discipline across *every* hop, sync and async, is the non-negotiable
foundation; everything else is reading the result.

## Types / Variants

### Spans and structure

- **Root span** — the top-level operation (the inbound request at the edge). Its duration is the
  end-to-end latency the user felt.
- **Child spans** — nested units of work (a downstream RPC, a DB query, a cache lookup), each
  pointing at its parent, forming the tree.
- **Span attributes** — key-value metadata on a span. *This is where high cardinality lives safely*:
  unlike metric labels, you *can* attach `user_id`, `order_id`, `build_sha`, or `region` to a span,
  because traces are sampled and stored as events, not as a cartesian product of time series. Rich
  attributes are what let you later filter "show traces for enterprise-tier users on build X."
- **Span events / links** — timestamped sub-events within a span, and links to spans in *other*
  traces (e.g. a batch job triggered by many requests).

### Context propagation

The mechanism that ties spans into one trace: the **W3C Trace Context** standard (`traceparent` /
`tracestate` headers) carries `trace_id` and parent `span_id` across HTTP, gRPC, and (via message
metadata) async queues. OpenTelemetry instruments most frameworks to inject and extract it
automatically.

### Sampling — the central cost lever

You cannot afford to store every trace at scale, so you keep a fraction. *Where* you decide is the
key distinction:

- **Head-based sampling** — decide at the *start* of the trace (at the root) whether to keep it,
  before you know how it turns out. Simple, cheap, stateless, and the decision propagates so the
  whole trace is consistently kept or dropped. Weakness: it's *blind to the outcome* — a purely
  random head sampler will, by chance, drop most of your rare errors and slow requests, which are
  exactly the traces you wanted.
- **Tail-based sampling** — buffer *all* spans of a trace until it completes, then decide *with full
  knowledge* — keep it if it errored, if it was slow (latency above a threshold), or if it touched a
  rare code path; drop the boring fast successes. Catches the interesting traces head sampling
  misses, at the cost of *buffering every trace in memory* until it finishes (significant resource
  and infrastructure cost) and needing a collector that sees all spans of a trace.

### Critical-path analysis

Not every span on the waterfall contributes to total latency. The **critical path** is the chain of
spans that actually determined the end-to-end duration — the serial dependencies whose sum *is* the
latency. Optimizing a span *off* the critical path (one running in parallel, finishing early)
changes nothing. Critical-path analysis is what tells you *which* slow span is worth fixing.

## When to use / When NOT

- **Use tracing when:** requests fan out across multiple services and you need to attribute latency
  or errors to a specific hop — the defining use case for microservices. **Don't bother when:** a
  single-process monolith where a local profiler/flamegraph already shows where time goes; tracing's
  cross-boundary value is wasted.
- **Use head sampling when:** you want simple, cheap, low-overhead tracing and a uniform random
  sample is acceptable for understanding *typical* behavior. **Avoid head sampling** as your only
  strategy if you must reliably capture rare errors/slow requests — randomness will drop most of
  them.
- **Use tail sampling when:** you specifically need to keep all errors and slow traces and can
  afford the buffering infrastructure. **Avoid tail sampling when:** trace volume is so high that
  buffering every trace to decide is prohibitively expensive — or combine: head-sample a baseline,
  tail-sample the interesting.
- **Put high-cardinality data on span attributes when:** you'll want to filter traces by it later
  (`user_id`, `build`, `region`) — traces are the *right* place for this, unlike metrics.
- **Invest in tracing when:** the operational cost is justified by genuinely cross-service latency
  problems. **Hold off when:** you have no distributed latency mystery to solve and the
  instrumentation + storage cost would buy nothing you'd query.

## Tradeoffs

The headline tradeoff is *where and how you sample*: completeness of the interesting traces versus
the cost of buffering and storing them.

| Decision | Buys you | Costs / risk |
| -------- | -------- | ------------ |
| Tracing at all | Cross-service latency attribution | Instrumentation effort + storage; wasted on a monolith |
| Head sampling | Cheap, simple, stateless, low overhead | Blind to outcome — randomly drops rare errors/slow traces |
| Tail sampling | Keeps all errors and slow traces | Must buffer every trace until complete — heavy infra cost |
| Rich span attributes | High-cardinality filtering after the fact | Larger spans; PII risk if you attach user data carelessly |
| 100% capture (no sampling) | Every trace available | Often unaffordable at scale; storage/cost explodes |

The recurring theme is **fidelity versus cost**, and sampling is the dial. The sophisticated answer
is rarely "head or tail" but *both*: a small uniform head sample to understand typical behavior
cheaply, plus tail sampling biased toward errors and high latency to guarantee the rare-but-critical
traces survive. Pick the dial by what you'll actually investigate — typical-case performance versus
worst-case incidents.

up wouldn't change end-to-end latency.

## Real-world examples

- **Google Dapper** is the foundational distributed-tracing system; its 2010 paper introduced
  spans, trace propagation, and sampling, and seeded essentially every tracer that followed.
- **OpenTelemetry** is now the vendor-neutral standard for trace instrumentation and context
  propagation (W3C Trace Context); the **OTel Collector** is where tail-based sampling is commonly
  configured. It superseded the earlier OpenTracing and OpenCensus projects.
- **Jaeger** (CNCF) and **Grafana Tempo** are the common open-source trace backends; Tempo pairs
  with Loki and Prometheus for correlated three-pillar debugging, and supports exemplar jumps from a
  metric straight into a trace.
- **Honeycomb and Lightstep** built their products around high-cardinality span attributes and
  tail-based sampling, making "filter all traces where `user.tier = enterprise` and latency > 1s"
  the core workflow — the case for putting rich attributes on spans rather than metric labels.
