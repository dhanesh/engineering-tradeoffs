## Overview

A **metric** is a numeric measurement aggregated over a time window — a request count, a
queue depth, a latency distribution. Metrics are the cheap, always-on pillar: their cost is
bounded by the number of distinct *label combinations*, not by traffic, so you can compute
them on every request and keep them for a year. That makes them the backbone of dashboards
and alerting. The discipline of metrics is twofold: picking the *right* instrument type for
each measurement (a counter, gauge, histogram, or summary — getting this wrong makes the data
meaningless or un-aggregatable), and picking the *right* signals to watch (the four golden
signals, RED, USE). The recurring failure mode is **cardinality explosion** — attaching a
high-cardinality label that multiplies your time series into the millions and bankrupts the
metrics backend. This page is about choosing instruments, choosing signals, and not blowing up
the cardinality budget.

## Mental model

**A metric is a pre-aggregated number with a low-cardinality set of labels; the moment you
want per-entity detail, you've left metrics-land.** Picture the metrics store as a giant
spreadsheet where each *row* is a unique combination of `(metric name, label values)` — one
**time series** — and each cell is that series' value at a point in time. The cost of the
whole system is the *number of rows*. Adding a label like `region` (5 values) to a metric with
3 existing series gives 15 series — fine. Adding `user_id` (10 million values) gives 30 million
series — catastrophe. So the mental rule is brutal and simple: **labels must be low-cardinality
and bounded.** `status_code`, `method`, `region`, `endpoint-template` — yes. `user_id`,
`request_id`, raw `url`, `email` — never. When you genuinely need to slice by a high-cardinality
dimension, that is a *logs or traces* question, not a metrics one.

Second model: **always measure the distribution, never just the average.** An average latency
of 200ms can hide that 1% of users wait 8 seconds — and that 1% is often your most valuable or
most vocal users. Averages are statistically meaningless for latency because the distribution is
heavily right-skewed: a few huge values barely move the mean but define the user experience. You
care about **percentiles** — p50 (typical), p95, p99, p99.9 (the tail). "p99 latency is 1.2s"
means 1% of requests are slower than 1.2s, which is a real, actionable statement about real
users. That is why latency metrics are **histograms**, not gauges of an average.

## Types / Variants

### Instrument types

- **Counter.** A value that only ever *increases* (or resets to zero on restart): total
  requests served, total bytes sent, total errors. You never read a counter's raw value; you
  read its *rate* of increase (`rate(http_requests_total[5m])`). Counters are monotonic so that
  rates survive restarts and are correctly summable across instances. Use for: "how many / how
  often."
- **Gauge.** A value that can go *up or down*: current memory in use, queue depth, in-flight
  requests, temperature. You read the instantaneous value, and aggregate with `avg`/`max`/`sum`
  as appropriate. Use for: "how much, right now."
- **Histogram.** Bucketed counts of observations, used to compute **percentiles**: latency,
  request size. The application pre-defines bucket boundaries (e.g. ≤10ms, ≤50ms, ≤100ms, …) and
  increments the bucket each observation falls into. Crucially, **histogram buckets are
  *aggregatable across instances*** — you can sum bucket counts from 50 servers and compute a
  correct *global* p99. This is the right choice for latency in a distributed system.
- **Summary.** Like a histogram, but the client computes **quantiles locally** (e.g. it tracks a
  streaming p99 per instance) and ships the quantile directly. The catch: **summary quantiles
  are NOT aggregatable** — you cannot average two instances' p99s to get the fleet p99 (a p99 of
  a p99 is mathematically wrong). Summaries are cheaper to query (no bucket math) and more
  accurate per-instance, but useless for fleet-wide percentiles. Prefer histograms unless you
  truly only need per-instance quantiles.

### Signal frameworks

- **The four golden signals** (Google SRE): **Latency** (how long requests take — split success
  from error latency), **Traffic** (demand on the system — requests/sec), **Errors** (rate of
  failed requests), **Saturation** (how full the system is — the most constrained resource's
  utilization, the leading indicator of imminent trouble). If you watch only four things, watch
  these.
- **RED** (request-scoped, for services): **Rate**, **Errors**, **Duration**. A focused subset
  of the golden signals aimed at request-driven services — the right starting dashboard for any
  microservice. (It drops saturation, which RED users get from USE on the underlying resources.)
- **USE** (resource-scoped, for infrastructure, from Brendan Gregg): for every *resource*
  (CPU, memory, disk, network, connection pool), track **Utilization** (% busy), **Saturation**
  (queued/waiting work), and **Errors**. RED tells you the service is sick; USE tells you which
  *resource* is the bottleneck.

## When to use / When NOT

- **Use a counter when:** the quantity only grows and you care about its rate (requests, errors,
  bytes). **Don't use a gauge** for these — you'd lose rate semantics across restarts and can't
  sum correctly.
- **Use a gauge when:** the value moves both directions and the instantaneous level matters
  (queue depth, memory, in-flight count). **Don't use a counter** — it can't go down.
- **Use a histogram when:** you need percentiles *and* must aggregate across many instances
  (latency in any distributed system). **Avoid a summary** there — its quantiles can't be merged.
- **Use a summary when:** you need an accurate per-instance quantile cheaply and will never
  aggregate it across instances. **Avoid it** as your fleet-wide latency SLI.
- **Use RED when:** instrumenting a request-driven service — it's the fastest path to a useful
  dashboard. **Use USE when:** diagnosing *which resource* is the bottleneck. **Use the golden
  signals when:** you want the canonical four-signal service health view.
- **Avoid high-cardinality labels — always.** Never put `user_id`, `request_id`, raw URLs, or
  unbounded IDs on a metric label. That detail belongs on logs/traces.

## Tradeoffs

The instrument choice is mostly determined by the measurement's shape and whether you must
aggregate; the headline pitfall is cardinality.

| Instrument | Measures | Aggregatable across instances? | Cost / pitfall |
| ---------- | -------- | ------------------------------ | -------------- |
| Counter | Monotonic totals (rate) | Yes (sum, then rate) | Cheap; misuse = using raw value not rate |
| Gauge | Up-and-down level | Yes (avg/max/sum) | Cheap; sampling can miss spikes between scrapes |
| Histogram | Distribution → percentiles | **Yes** (sum buckets) | More series (one per bucket); pick buckets well |
| Summary | Per-instance quantiles | **No** | Can't compute fleet percentiles; quantiles fixed at instrument time |

Two cross-cutting tradeoffs dominate. First, **cardinality versus insight**: more labels let you
slice finer but multiply series count multiplicatively — the cost is super-linear, and one
unbounded label can take a metrics backend down. Budget cardinality like memory. Second, **pull
versus push** for collection: a *pull* model (Prometheus scrapes targets on an interval) gives
the monitoring system control over rate, free liveness detection (a target that fails to scrape
is down), and easy service discovery — but struggles with short-lived jobs and targets behind
NAT/firewalls. A *push* model (the app pushes to a gateway/collector — StatsD, OTLP push, the
Prometheus Pushgateway) handles ephemeral batch jobs and locked-down networks, at the cost of
losing free liveness signal and needing the app to know where to send. Most stacks pull
long-lived services and push only short-lived jobs.

## Real-world examples

- **Prometheus** is the reference metrics system: a *pull* model with a multi-dimensional data
  model (`metric{label=value}`), the four instrument types above, and PromQL for rates and
  histogram-quantile math. The **Pushgateway** exists specifically for short-lived jobs that
  can't be scraped.
- **Google's four golden signals** (SRE book) and **Brendan Gregg's USE method** are the two
  canonical signal frameworks; **RED** (Tom Wilkie, Weaveworks/Grafana) is the request-scoped
  distillation widely used for microservice dashboards.
- **StatsD / Graphite** popularized the *push* model with client-side aggregation; **OpenTelemetry
  metrics** with OTLP support both push and pull and is the emerging vendor-neutral standard.
- **Cardinality explosion** is the canonical metrics outage: a deploy that adds `user_id` or a
  raw URL as a label can multiply series into the millions overnight — every managed vendor
  (Datadog, Grafana, Chronosphere) bills on and rate-limits active time series for exactly this
  reason.
