## Overview

**Monitoring tells you *that* something is wrong; observability tells you *why*.**
Monitoring is the practice of watching a *predefined* set of signals — dashboards
and alerts built around failure modes you already anticipated. Observability is a
property of the system: the degree to which you can understand its internal state
from its external outputs *without shipping new code*. The difference matters most
when something breaks in a way nobody predicted. A monitored system answers the
questions you asked in advance; an observable system lets you ask new questions of
data already being emitted. This cluster covers the three pillars that produce that
data — **metrics**, **logs**, and **traces** — how to unify them, how to keep their
cost from exploding, and how to use them as a *debugging loop* rather than a wall of
graphs. The SLO discipline that decides *what* to measure and alert on lives in the
Reliability cluster; this cluster is about the telemetry that feeds it.

## Mental model

**Telemetry is evidence; observability is the ability to run an investigation on it.**
Two distinctions anchor the whole field:

The first is **known-unknowns versus unknown-unknowns**. A known-unknown is a failure
mode you can name in advance — "the database might run out of connections" — so you
pre-build a dashboard and an alert for it. That is monitoring, and it is necessary but
finite: you can only pre-build for failures you imagined. An unknown-unknown is the
failure you never thought of — "checkout is slow, but only for users in Mumbai paying
with one specific card network, and only since the 14:00 deploy." You cannot dashboard
your way to that in advance. Observability is what lets you slice the data along
*arbitrary* dimensions after the fact to corner it. The dividing line is whether you
must ship new instrumentation to answer the question: if yes, the system was not
observable for that question.

The second is **cardinality** — the number of distinct values a dimension can take.
`http_status` has low cardinality (a handful of codes); `user_id` or `request_id` has
near-unbounded cardinality (millions). High-cardinality data is exactly what you need
to corner an unknown-unknown (drill down to the one customer, one build, one host), and
it is exactly what is expensive to store and index. The entire economics of
observability — metrics versus logs versus traces, sampling, retention — is a fight
over how much high-cardinality detail you can afford to keep. That tension is the
recurring theme across every page in this cluster.

## Types / Variants

**The three pillars** are three complementary shapes of telemetry, each strong where
the others are weak:

- **Metrics** — numeric measurements aggregated over time (a request counter, a latency
  histogram, CPU saturation). Cheap, compact, and ideal for *dashboards and alerting*
  because cost is bounded by the number of distinct label combinations, not by traffic
  volume. Weak at high cardinality: you cannot put `user_id` on a metric without a
  cardinality explosion.
- **Logs** — timestamped records of discrete events, ideally **structured** (key-value
  JSON, not free text). Rich, high-cardinality, and ideal for *the detailed story of a
  single event*. Cost scales with event volume, so logs get expensive and must be
  sampled or retention-capped.
- **Traces** — the causal, end-to-end path of *one request* as it fans out across
  services, broken into timed **spans**. Ideal for *latency attribution in distributed
  systems* — "which of the eleven services on this request was slow?" Cost is dominated
  by volume, so traces are almost always sampled.

**Their unification** is the whole game. The three pillars are far more than the sum of
their parts when *correlated* by shared identifiers — a **trace ID** and **span ID**
threaded through every log line and attached to metrics as **exemplars**. With that
thread, the debugging loop becomes: a metric tells you *something* spiked, an exemplar
jumps you from the spiking point on the graph to a representative *trace*, the trace
shows *which span* is slow, and that span's *logs* (joined by trace ID) tell you exactly
*why*. Three disconnected tools become one navigable surface. **OpenTelemetry (OTel)** is
the vendor-neutral standard — APIs, SDKs, and the OTel Collector — that emits all three
pillars with consistent semantic conventions and propagates that correlating context, so
you are not locked into one vendor's agent or wire format.

## When to use / When NOT

- **Invest in observability when:** you run a distributed or microservice system where
  failures cross service boundaries and you genuinely face unknown-unknowns. **Don't
  over-invest when:** a single-process monolith with a handful of known failure modes —
  basic metrics plus structured logs may be all you act on; a full tracing stack is
  overhead nobody queries.
- **Reach for metrics when:** you need cheap, always-on dashboards and alerts on
  aggregate health (the four golden signals). **Avoid metrics when:** you need to slice
  by a high-cardinality dimension — that's a logs or traces question.
- **Reach for logs when:** you need the full detailed context of individual events
  (the request body, the exact error, the user). **Avoid relying on logs alone** for
  cross-service latency — you'll be grepping timestamps across services by hand; that's
  what traces are for.
- **Reach for traces when:** latency or errors span multiple services and you must
  attribute them to one hop. **Avoid full-fidelity tracing when:** volume makes 100%
  capture unaffordable — sample, and accept that you keep the interesting traces, not
  all of them.
- **Adopt OpenTelemetry when:** you want vendor-neutral instrumentation and the freedom
  to switch backends. **Skip the migration churn when:** you're tiny and a single
  managed agent already does everything you need — though new instrumentation should
  still default to OTel.

## Tradeoffs

The pillars trade cost, cardinality, and the kind of question they answer. Choose by the
question, not by fashion; mature stacks run all three and correlate them.

| Pillar | Best at | Cardinality | Cost driver | Weak at |
| ------ | ------- | ----------- | ----------- | ------- |
| Metrics | Dashboards, alerting, trends | Low (label sets) | Distinct label combinations | High-cardinality drill-down |
| Logs | The detailed story of one event | High | Event volume × size | Cross-service latency attribution |
| Traces | Latency attribution across services | High (sampled) | Span volume | Aggregate trends (sampling skews) |

The master tradeoff mirrors the rest of the field: **fidelity versus cost.** Every dimension
of detail you keep — every label on a metric, every retained log line, every unsampled trace —
buys you the ability to answer a future question, and costs storage, indexing, and money. The
art is keeping *enough* high-cardinality evidence to corner unknown-unknowns without paying to
store everything. Sampling, retention policies, and cardinality budgets are how you set that
dial — covered in detail on the metrics, tracing, and dashboards-and-debugging pages.

## Real-world examples

- **Google** originated much of the practice — Dapper (the distributed-tracing paper that
  seeded the field), Borgmon (the metrics system Prometheus is modeled on), and the SRE books
  that codified golden signals and SLO-driven alerting.
- **Prometheus + Grafana** is the de facto open-source metrics-and-dashboards stack; pairing it
  with **Loki** (logs) and **Tempo** (traces) gives a correlated three-pillar stack from one
  vendor, all OpenTelemetry-compatible.
- **Honeycomb** popularized the high-cardinality, event-driven take on observability — wide
  structured events you can slice arbitrarily — and is the canonical argument that
  unknown-unknowns demand cardinality that classic metrics can't carry.
- **Datadog, New Relic, and Grafana Cloud** are the mainstream managed platforms that unify all
  three pillars with correlation and exemplars built in; **OpenTelemetry** is the standard they
  all now ingest, decoupling instrumentation from backend choice.
