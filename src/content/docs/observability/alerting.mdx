## Overview

An **alert** is the bridge from telemetry to a human: it decides *when a person must be
interrupted*. That makes alerting the highest-stakes, easiest-to-get-wrong part of observability.
Too few alerts and real outages go unnoticed; too many and you get **alert fatigue** — the slow
poisoning where on-call engineers, drowning in noise, start ignoring pages and miss the one that
mattered. The whole discipline reduces to a single principle: **page a human only for things that
are both urgent and actionable, and ideally only for things users can feel.** This page covers the
core decisions — alerting on **symptoms** (user-visible pain) versus **causes** (internal
conditions), alerting on **SLO burn rate** (the right primary signal, covered in depth in the
Reliability cluster), static **thresholds** versus **anomaly detection**, the routing split between
**paging** and **ticketing**, attaching **runbooks**, the **good-alert checklist**, and the
**dead man's switch** that catches the silent failure of the alerting system itself.

## Mental model

**Alert on symptoms the user feels, not on causes you happened to anticipate.** A *symptom* alert
fires on the thing the user actually experiences: "the checkout success rate dropped below 99%,"
"p99 latency exceeds 2s." A *cause* alert fires on an internal condition: "CPU is at 90%," "a
replica is down," "the queue is deep." The trap is that there are *infinitely many* causes — you
will never enumerate them all, and most of them don't actually hurt users (CPU at 90% with latency
fine is not a problem). Symptom-based alerting flips this: there are *few* symptoms (the golden
signals), each one *definitely* matters because it's user-visible, and a symptom alert catches
*every* cause that produces it — including the unknown-unknowns you never thought to alert on.
Causes belong on **dashboards** you consult *after* a symptom pages you, to diagnose; they rarely
belong on a pager. The refinement of this principle is to alert on **SLO burn rate** — page when
you're consuming your error budget too fast — which is symptom-based by construction and tunes
precision/recall far better than a raw threshold (the full burn-rate recipe lives in the
*SLOs, SLIs & error budgets* page).

Second model: **every page is a withdrawal from a finite trust account.** Each alert that fires and
turns out to be noise — a flap, a non-actionable warning, a cause that didn't hurt anyone — spends
the on-call engineer's trust in the alerting system. Spend enough and they tune it out entirely,
and now your alerting is *worse than none*, because it creates false confidence while real pages get
ignored. So the bar for *paging* a human is severe: it must be urgent (needs action *now*, not
tomorrow), actionable (there's something the human can do), and real (a user is being hurt or about
to be). Everything that fails that bar should *ticket*, not page.

## Types / Variants

### Symptom vs cause

- **Symptom alerts** — fire on user-visible degradation (error rate, latency, the golden signals,
  SLO burn). Few, high-value, catch unknown causes. *This is what should page.*
- **Cause alerts** — fire on internal conditions (resource saturation, a dependency down). Useful as
  *diagnostic* signals and dashboards; a worthy page only when the cause is a near-certain *leading
  indicator* of imminent user pain (e.g. disk will fill in 30 minutes).

### Threshold strategies

- **Static thresholds** — "alert if X > N." Simple, predictable, explainable. The right default.
  Weakness: a single number rarely fits a metric with daily/weekly seasonality (traffic is
  naturally 10× higher at noon than 3am), causing false alarms or missed dips.
- **Anomaly detection** — learns the normal pattern (including seasonality) and alerts on
  *deviation*. Handles seasonal metrics where a static line can't. Weakness: opaque ("why did it
  fire?"), can be noisy, harder to trust and tune, needs enough clean history. Use it where
  seasonality genuinely defeats a static threshold, not as a default.
- **Burn-rate alerts (multi-window)** — alert on the *rate of error-budget consumption*, pairing a
  fast-burn window (page now) with a slow-burn window (ticket). The SRE-recommended primary signal;
  high precision and high recall. Detailed in the Reliability cluster.

### Routing: paging vs ticketing

- **Paging** — interrupts a human *now* (push, SMS, phone call via PagerDuty/Opsgenie). Reserved for
  urgent + actionable + user-impacting.
- **Ticketing** — files work for the next business day (a ticket, a Slack channel, an email digest).
  For things that need attention but not at 3am — slow burns, warnings, degraded-but-serving.

### Supporting pieces

- **Runbook** — a linked, step-by-step procedure attached to every paging alert: what it means, how
  to confirm, how to mitigate, when to escalate. An alert without a runbook makes a half-awake
  engineer reinvent the response under pressure.
- **The good-alert checklist** — before an alert pages, it must pass: (1) **actionable** — there's a
  defined response; (2) **urgent** — it needs doing *now*; (3) **user-impacting** — symptom-based,
  someone is being hurt; (4) **has a runbook**; (5) **not flappy** — won't oscillate on noise (needs
  a `for`/duration clause and a confirmation window). Fail any and it should ticket, not page.
- **Dead man's switch** — a heartbeat alert that *expects* a regular signal and fires when it
  *stops*. It catches the catastrophic silent failure: your monitoring pipeline itself is down, so
  *no* alerts can fire and everything looks deceptively quiet. The dead man's switch is the alert
  that watches the watchers.

## When to use / When NOT

- **Alert on symptoms when:** deciding what should page — almost always. **Avoid paging on causes**
  unless the cause is a near-certain leading indicator of imminent user pain; otherwise it's a
  dashboard signal, not a page.
- **Alert on SLO burn rate when:** you have an SLO and a budget — make it your primary user-facing
  alert. **Avoid raw static error-rate pages** as the primary signal; they trade precision against
  recall badly (see the Reliability cluster).
- **Use static thresholds when:** the metric is stable or you can set a clear bound — the explainable
  default. **Use anomaly detection when:** strong seasonality genuinely defeats a static line.
  **Avoid anomaly detection** as a default — its opacity erodes trust and it's hard to tune.
- **Page when:** the condition is urgent, actionable, and user-impacting. **Ticket instead when:**
  it's important but not now (slow burns, warnings, non-urgent degradation) — paging it causes
  fatigue.
- **Attach a runbook when:** any alert can page — always. **Never ship a paging alert without one.**
- **Add a dead man's switch when:** you depend on alerts firing — always. **Don't assume silence
  means health**; silence might mean your monitoring is dead.
- **Cut an alert when:** it has paged and been actioned-as-noise repeatedly. A page nobody acts on is
  pure fatigue — delete it or demote it to a ticket.

## Tradeoffs

The master tension is **precision versus recall** — catch every real incident (recall) without
crying wolf (precision) — and every choice tunes that dial against on-call sanity.

| Decision | Buys you | Costs / risk |
| -------- | -------- | ------------ |
| Symptom alerts (page) | Few, high-value, catch unknown causes | Slightly later detection than a leading-indicator cause |
| Cause alerts (page) | Early warning on a known failure mode | Infinite causes, mostly non-impacting → fatigue |
| Static threshold | Simple, explainable, predictable | Fails on seasonal metrics; tuning is manual |
| Anomaly detection | Handles seasonality automatically | Opaque, noisy, hard to trust/tune, needs history |
| Paging | Immediate human response | Interrupts a life; overuse = alert fatigue |
| Ticketing | No 3am interruption for non-urgent work | Slower response; wrong for a true emergency |
| Dead man's switch | Catches silent monitoring failure | One more alert to maintain (cheap, worth it) |

The dominant failure mode is **alert fatigue**: optimizing recall in isolation (alert on everything)
destroys precision, engineers tune out, and effective recall collapses to zero. The whole craft is
keeping the *paging* set small, symptom-based, actionable, and runbook-backed — and routing
everything else to tickets.

## Diagram

The alert routing pipeline: telemetry to evaluation to the page-or-ticket decision, with a dead
man's switch watching that the pipeline itself is alive.

<Mermaid code={`graph TD
  TEL[Telemetry<br/>metrics / SLO burn] --> EVAL{Alert rule fires?<br/>with 'for' duration}
  EVAL -->|no| OK[Steady state]
  EVAL -->|yes| GATE{Urgent + actionable<br/>+ user-impacting?}
  GATE -->|yes — symptom / fast burn| PAGE[PAGE on-call now<br/>+ linked runbook]
  GATE -->|no — slow burn / warning / cause| TICKET[File a ticket<br/>handle next business day]
  PAGE --> RUN[Runbook: confirm, mitigate, escalate]
  HB[Heartbeat from pipeline] --> DMS{Heartbeat still arriving?}
  DMS -->|stopped| DEADPAGE[Dead man's switch PAGES<br/>monitoring itself is down]`} />

A rule fires only after a sustained `for` duration (no flapping). The gate routes user-impacting
urgent symptoms to a page with an attached runbook, and everything else to a ticket. Independently, a
dead man's switch watches for a heartbeat from the monitoring pipeline and pages if it stops —
catching the silent failure where no alerts can fire at all.

## Real-world examples

- **Google SRE** codified symptom-based alerting and the move away from cause-based pages; *My
  Philosophy on Alerting* (Rob Ewaschuk) is the foundational argument that you should page on
  symptoms and user pain, not on every internal condition.
- **PagerDuty and Opsgenie** are the standard paging/on-call routers — escalation policies,
  schedules, and the paging-versus-ticketing split — integrating with Prometheus Alertmanager,
  Datadog, and the rest.
- **Prometheus Alertmanager** implements the `for` duration (anti-flap), routing trees, grouping,
  inhibition (suppress a flood of cause alerts when a symptom alert already fired), and silences —
  the open-source embodiment of the routing model on this page.
- **Dead Man's Snitch / Healthchecks.io** and Alertmanager's `Watchdog`/`DeadMansSwitch` pattern are
  the canonical heartbeat tools that page when the *expected* signal stops — guarding against silent
  monitoring failure.
