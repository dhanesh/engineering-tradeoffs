## Overview

**Coordination** is the layer where independent nodes are made to act as one organized
system: agreeing on *who is in charge* (leader election), *who is allowed to act*
(distributed locks), *who exists* (membership), *where to find a service* (discovery),
and *how a multi-node operation commits or aborts atomically* (distributed
transactions). Almost all of it reduces to consensus underneath — which is why a small
number of consensus-backed coordination services (**ZooKeeper, etcd, Consul**) sit at
the heart of so many architectures, providing a tiny, linearizable, strongly-consistent
store that everything else coordinates *through*. This page covers those primitives, the
coordination services that provide them, and the failure modes that make naïve
coordination dangerous — the **thundering herd**, **split-brain**, and the
**fencing-token** fix — plus the spectrum of distributed-transaction protocols (2PC,
3PC, saga, TCC) and why blocking commit protocols are so often the wrong default.

## Mental model

**Don't coordinate in the application; coordinate through a consensus store.** The
durable mental model is a **single source of truth** — a small, strongly-consistent,
highly-available service (etcd/ZooKeeper/Consul) that holds the *one* authoritative
answer to "who's the leader / who holds the lock / who's alive / where is service X."
Your nodes never try to agree *peer-to-peer* about these things (that's hard consensus
you shouldn't re-implement); they all defer to the store, which has already solved
consensus correctly.

The second load-bearing idea: **a distributed lock is not a mutex.** A process can
acquire a lock, then pause (GC, VM migration, network blip) *past its lease expiry*
without knowing it; the lock service grants the lock to someone else; now **two**
processes believe they hold it. A lock alone cannot prevent the stalled process from
acting on stale belief. The fix is a **fencing token**: every lock grant carries a
monotonically increasing number, and the *protected resource* rejects any operation
carrying a token lower than the highest it has seen. The lock service provides mutual
exclusion *most of the time*; the fencing token makes it *safe even when it fails*. Any
distributed-lock design without fencing has a correctness hole.

Third: **atomic commit across nodes is fundamentally harder than consensus, because it
requires *unanimity*, not majority.** A transaction commits only if *all* participants
can — so a single crashed participant can block everyone (2PC's defining weakness).
That tension is why modern systems often abandon distributed ACID transactions for
**sagas** (sequences of local transactions with compensations) that trade atomicity for
availability.

## Types / Variants

### Coordination primitives

- **Leader election.** Elect exactly one coordinator among peers. Implemented by
  creating a single well-known key in the consensus store (the node that creates it
  wins) with a lease/ephemeral lifetime so the leadership is released if the leader
  dies. Used for single-writer designs, schedulers, and failover.
- **Distributed lock / lease.** Mutual exclusion across nodes, almost always as a
  **time-bounded lease** (auto-released on expiry) so a dead holder can't lock the
  resource forever — paired with a **fencing token** for safety.
- **Service discovery.** A registry mapping logical service names to current healthy
  endpoints, so callers find providers despite churn. Push (clients watch for changes)
  or pull (clients query/DNS). etcd/Consul + health checks, or Kubernetes Services.
- **Membership.** The agreed set of live nodes in a cluster. Strong membership via the
  consensus store, or scalable eventually-consistent membership via **gossip/SWIM**
  (Consul, Serf, Cassandra) when exact agreement isn't required.

### Coordination services

- **ZooKeeper** (ZAB). The veteran. A hierarchical namespace of **znodes** with
  **ephemeral** nodes (vanish when the session dies — perfect for locks/leases) and
  **watches** (notify on change). Mature, battle-tested, used by Kafka (historically),
  HBase, Hadoop. Java; heavier to operate.
- **etcd** (Raft). The Kubernetes backbone. A flat key-value store with leases,
  watches, and atomic compare-and-swap; clean gRPC API. The default for cloud-native
  coordination.
- **Consul** (Raft + gossip). Coordination *plus* first-class service discovery, health
  checking, and a service mesh. Uses Raft for the strongly-consistent catalog and
  **SWIM gossip** for scalable membership/failure detection.

### The failure modes

- **Thundering herd (the herd effect).** When a contended resource frees (a lock
  releases, a leader dies), *every* waiter wakes and rushes at once, overwhelming the
  store or the resource. Fixes: **sequential/queue locks** (each waiter watches only its
  immediate predecessor, so exactly one wakes — ZooKeeper's lock recipe), randomized
  backoff, and watch-fan-out limits.
- **Split-brain.** A partition lets *two* nodes both believe they're the leader (or
  primary), each accepting writes → divergence/corruption. Prevented by **quorum**
  (only the majority side can hold leadership; the minority steps down) and made *safe*
  by **fencing tokens** (stale leader's writes are rejected downstream).
- **Fencing tokens.** The monotonic counter that turns "mostly-correct mutual exclusion"
  into "safe mutual exclusion." Essential whenever a lock guards an external resource
  (storage, a database) that can't itself participate in the consensus.

### Distributed transactions (atomic commit across services)

- **2PC (Two-Phase Commit).** A coordinator runs **prepare** (all participants vote
  commit/abort and durably promise) then **commit/abort** (apply the unanimous
  decision). Gives atomicity, but is a **blocking** protocol: if the coordinator crashes
  after participants vote *yes* but before delivering the decision, participants hold
  locks **indefinitely**, unsure whether to commit. A single coordinator failure can
  freeze the system. Synchronous, lock-heavy, latency-bound.
- **3PC (Three-Phase Commit).** Adds a **pre-commit** phase so participants can reach a
  safe default and avoid indefinite blocking under a coordinator crash — but it assumes
  bounded network delay (synchrony) and is **unsafe under network partitions**, so it's
  rarely used in practice. It trades 2PC's blocking for an extra round and partition
  fragility.
- **Saga.** A long-running transaction split into a sequence of **local** transactions,
  each with a **compensating** action that semantically undoes it. On failure, run the
  compensations in reverse. No global locks, no blocking, stays available — but only
  **eventual** atomicity, no isolation (intermediate states are visible), and you must
  design idempotent, commutative compensations. Orchestrated (a coordinator drives the
  steps) or choreographed (events trigger the next step). The default for
  microservices.
- **TCC (Try-Confirm/Cancel).** An application-level 2PC: **Try** reserves resources
  (e.g. hold inventory), then **Confirm** finalizes or **Cancel** releases. Like a saga
  with an explicit reservation step, giving more isolation than a raw saga without
  database-level distributed locks. Used in payments/booking where a "reserve then
  settle" model is natural.

## When to use / When NOT

- **Use a consensus coordination service when:** you need leader election, locks,
  membership, config, or discovery with correctness guarantees. **Don't build these
  peer-to-peer** in your app — defer to etcd/ZooKeeper/Consul.
- **Use distributed locks + fencing tokens when:** exactly one node may act on a shared
  external resource. **Never ship a distributed lock without a fencing token** if the
  resource can't reject stale holders.
- **Use gossip/SWIM membership when:** the cluster is large and *exact* agreement on
  membership isn't required (failure detection, discovery hints). **Use the consensus
  store** when membership must be authoritative (who's in the Raft group).
- **Use 2PC only when:** participants are within one trust/admin domain, are all
  XA-capable, and you can tolerate its blocking and latency (intra-DB cross-shard
  commits). **Avoid 2PC across microservices / the internet** — coordinator failure
  blocks everyone and locks don't survive service autonomy.
- **Avoid 3PC** in real networks — its synchrony assumption breaks under partition;
  prefer a saga or consensus-based commit.
- **Use sagas when:** a business operation spans multiple services and availability
  beats strict isolation (order → payment → shipping). **Avoid sagas when:**
  intermediate visibility is unacceptable or you can't write correct compensations.
- **Use TCC when:** a natural reserve/settle flow exists (payments, seat/inventory
  booking) and you want more isolation than a bare saga.

## Tradeoffs

| Distributed-txn protocol | Atomicity | Isolation | Availability | Blocking risk |
| ------------------------ | --------- | --------- | ------------ | ------------- |
| 2PC | Strong (all-or-nothing) | Strong (locks held) | Low — coordinator is SPOF | **High** (coordinator crash blocks) |
| 3PC | Strong (no indefinite block) | Strong | Low–medium | Lower, but **partition-unsafe** |
| Saga | Eventual (compensations) | **None** (intermediates visible) | High | None |
| TCC | Eventual (confirm/cancel) | Medium (reservations) | High | None |

The defining tradeoff across coordination: **strong coordination (consensus, 2PC) buys
correctness with unavailability and latency; relaxed coordination (gossip, saga) buys
availability and scale by giving up atomicity/isolation.** And for *every* lock-based
design, the non-negotiable: mutual exclusion is best-effort; **fencing tokens** are what
make it actually safe.

**quorum + fencing tokens** prevent when the elected leader stalls during a partition.

  S-->>B: /leader deleted (A's lease gone)
  B->>S: create /leader => WINS, token=43
  Note over B: B is leader (token 43)
  A->>R: write (stale, carries token=42)
  R-->>A: REJECT (42 < highest seen 43)

Quorum/lease ensures only one *current* leader; the **fencing token** makes the system
safe even in the window where the old leader (A) hasn't realized it lost leadership —
the resource rejects its stale write. Without the token, A and B would both write and
corrupt the resource.

## Real-world examples

- **Kubernetes** stores all cluster state in **etcd** (Raft) and uses leader election
  (via etcd leases) so exactly one controller-manager/scheduler is active — textbook
  consensus-backed coordination.
- **Apache Kafka** historically used **ZooKeeper** for controller election, broker
  membership, and config (now replaced by Raft-based **KRaft**). HBase and Hadoop YARN
  still rely on ZooKeeper for master election and coordination.
- **HashiCorp Consul** powers service discovery and health-checked service meshes,
  combining Raft (consistent catalog) with SWIM gossip (scalable failure detection).
- **The Martin Kleppmann "How to do distributed locking" analysis** is the canonical
  argument for **fencing tokens** — showing how a GC pause breaks a Redis/Redlock lock
  and how a monotonic fencing token at the storage layer fixes it.
- **Microservice e-commerce checkouts** routinely use **sagas** (order service emits
  events; payment and shipping react, with compensations like refund/cancel on
  failure), and **payment gateways** use **TCC**-style authorize-then-capture
  (reserve funds, then settle or release).
