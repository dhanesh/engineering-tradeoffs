## Overview

Every other cluster in this primer is about *designing* a system; this one is about
*getting it into production and keeping it running*. Delivery and operations is the
machinery between a commit on a laptop and bytes serving real users — the build, the
tests, the artifact, the deploy, the rollback, and the people who own the result at 3am.
It is where architecture meets reality: a beautifully designed service that takes two
weeks and a change-advisory board to ship is, in practice, a slow and fragile system.

The central claim of modern delivery — backed by a decade of **DORA** (DevOps Research
and Assessment) research — is counter-intuitive to traditional ops: **shipping smaller
changes more often makes you *more* stable, not less.** Large, infrequent releases batch
up risk; small, frequent ones shrink the blast radius of any one change and make the
cause of a failure obvious. Speed and stability are not a tradeoff you balance — the same
practices (automation, small batches, fast feedback) buy you both. This page frames the
whole cluster: how to *measure* delivery (DORA), the spectrum from continuous integration
to continuous deployment, the deployment pipeline that connects them, and the ownership
model — **you build it, you run it** — that closes the loop between the people who write
code and the people who operate it.

## Mental model

**Delivery is a flow problem, and your job is to maximize the rate of small, safe changes
reaching production.** Think of every change as a unit of work moving down a pipe: commit
to build to test to deploy. Three things matter: how *fast* a change flows (lead time),
how *often* changes flow (deploy frequency), and what fraction *break* on the way out
(change-fail rate) plus how fast you *recover* when they do (MTTR). Those four are the
**DORA metrics**, and they are not vanity numbers — they correlate with organizational
performance, and crucially they form **two pairs that move together, not against each
other**: throughput (frequency + lead time) and stability (change-fail rate + MTTR).
Elite teams are good at *both*, because the same lever — small batches automated end to
end — improves all four.

The second model is the **CI / CD / CD ladder**, three escalating levels of automation
that are constantly conflated:

- **Continuous Integration (CI):** every commit is automatically merged to mainline and
  *verified* (build + tests + scans). The discipline: integrate small and often so
  conflicts and breakages surface in minutes, not at a painful end-of-sprint merge.
- **Continuous Delivery (CD):** every change that passes CI is automatically taken all
  the way to a *release-ready, deployable artifact* — but the final push to production is
  a **human decision** (a button click). You are *always able* to ship; you choose *when*.
- **Continuous Deployment (CD):** the same pipeline, but the human is removed — every
  change that passes all gates **deploys to production automatically**. No button.

The third model: **the deployment pipeline is your immune system.** Each stage is a gate
that a change must pass before progressing, and gates get more expensive and more
production-like as you go (fast unit tests first, slow end-to-end and manual approvals
last) — **fail fast and cheap.** A change that fails a 20-second unit test should never
reach a 20-minute integration suite. The fourth and most cultural model is **you build
it, you run it** (Werner Vogels, Amazon): the team that writes a service also operates it,
carries its pager, and feels its production pain. This is not just an org chart — it is a
*feedback loop* that aligns incentives. Developers insulated from operations build
operationally hostile systems; developers who get paged at 3am build systems that don't
page.

## Types / Variants

The delivery automation spectrum, from least to most automated:

- **Manual / scripted deploys.** A human runs steps (or a script) to release. No
  enforced gates. Fine for a side project; a liability at scale — error-prone, slow,
  and tribal-knowledge-bound.
- **Continuous Integration (CI).** Automated build + test on every commit to mainline.
  The non-negotiable foundation: catches integration breakage in minutes. *Stops at a
  green build* — does not necessarily produce a deployable artifact or deploy anything.
- **Continuous Delivery (CD).** CI plus automated packaging and promotion through
  environments to a *release-ready* state, with a **manual gate** before production.
  Every commit is *deployable*; releasing is a business decision (a click).
- **Continuous Deployment (CD).** CD with the manual gate removed: pass the pipeline,
  go live automatically. Requires very high test confidence, robust monitoring, and
  fast automated rollback — the gates *are* the safety, since no human reviews each push.

Two related axes shape how the above behave:

- **Trunk-based vs feature-branch development.** CI works best with **trunk-based**
  development (everyone commits to mainline daily, behind feature flags). Long-lived
  feature branches defeat the *Integration* in Continuous Integration — see
  [CI/CD pipelines](/delivery/ci-cd-pipelines/).
- **Deploy vs release.** Decoupling *deploying* code (it's running in production) from
  *releasing* a feature (users can see it) — via **feature flags** — is what makes
  continuous deployment safe. You deploy constantly and dark; you release deliberately —
  see [deployment strategies](/delivery/deployment-strategies/).

## When to use / When NOT

- **Adopt CI for:** essentially every team with more than one engineer. There is no
  credible argument against automated build-and-test on every commit. **The only reason
  to skip it** is a throwaway prototype that will never be maintained.
- **Adopt Continuous Delivery when:** you want to *release* on a business cadence
  (marketing, compliance, customer comms) but still want every change deployable on
  demand. The manual gate is a *deliberate* product decision, not a lack of automation.
  **Most regulated and enterprise teams live here** — and that's correct.
- **Adopt Continuous Deployment when:** you have strong automated test coverage,
  comprehensive monitoring/alerting, fast automated rollback, and a culture of small
  changes. **Avoid it when:** a bad deploy has irreversible or high-stakes consequences
  (financial ledgers, medical, aviation) without the safety nets — or when your test
  suite can't be trusted to gate unattended. Removing the human before the safety nets
  exist just automates incidents.
- **Use DORA metrics to:** find your bottleneck (is lead time dominated by code review?
  by a slow test suite? by manual deploy steps?) and to track whether changes actually
  improve flow. **Don't weaponize them** as individual performance targets — they are
  *system* health metrics; gaming them (e.g. splitting changes to inflate deploy
  frequency) destroys their value.
- **Apply you-build-it-you-run-it when:** teams own services end to end and you want
  fast feedback aligning design with operability. **Be wary when:** on-call burden is
  unsustainable, or a dedicated platform/SRE team genuinely provides better leverage for
  shared infrastructure — ownership is a spectrum, not a religion.

## Tradeoffs

The CI to CD to CD progression trades *human control points* for *speed and consistency*.
Each step removes a manual checkpoint and replaces it with automated confidence — which
is only safe if the automated confidence is real.

| Dimension | Continuous Integration | Continuous Delivery | Continuous Deployment |
| --------- | ---------------------- | ------------------- | --------------------- |
| What's automated | Build + test on every commit | + package + promote to release-ready | + push to production |
| Production gate | N/A (no deploy) | **Manual** (human clicks deploy) | **None** (auto on green) |
| Prerequisite confidence | Good test suite | Strong suite + staging | Excellent suite + monitoring + auto-rollback |
| Lead time to prod | Hours–days (manual deploy) | Minutes–hours (on click) | Minutes (automatic) |
| Best fit | Every team | Regulated / business-cadence releases | High-trust, high-frequency teams |
| Main risk | Stops short of deployable | Manual gate can become a bottleneck | A bad change ships with no human in the loop |

The deeper tradeoff is **batch size**. Smaller batches mean more frequent deploys, lower
per-deploy risk, faster feedback, and easier debugging — but require investment in
automation and test reliability to keep the per-deploy *cost* near zero. Larger batches
amortize a slow, expensive deploy process but concentrate risk and make failures hard to
diagnose. The DORA finding is unambiguous: **reduce batch size and the cost of deploying,
and both speed and stability improve together.**

**MTTR** is how fast that loop closes. CI ends at a green build; Continuous Delivery adds
the manual production gate; Continuous Deployment removes it.

## Real-world examples

- **Amazon** popularized **you-build-it-you-run-it** (Werner Vogels) and famously deploys
  to production thousands of times per day across its services — the canonical proof that
  high frequency and high stability coexist when batches are small and automation is total.
- **The DORA / *Accelerate* research program** (Nicole Forsgren, Jez Humble, Gene Kim)
  established the four metrics and the statistically-validated link between delivery
  performance and organizational performance — the empirical backbone of this whole cluster.
- **Etsy** was an early public exemplar of continuous deployment, going from infrequent
  releases to dozens of deploys a day, and open-sourced much of the tooling and culture
  (feature flags, dashboards, blameless postmortems) that made it safe.
- **GitHub Flow / trunk-based development at Google** show the branching discipline that
  underpins real CI: short-lived branches or direct-to-trunk commits behind flags, merged
  daily, so integration is continuous rather than a quarterly event.
- **Netflix** built **Spinnaker** (open-sourced) to run continuous delivery with automated
  canary analysis at scale, encoding progressive delivery and automated rollback into the
  pipeline itself.
