## Overview

A cache has finite memory, so when it fills up and a new entry needs space, it must
*evict* an existing one. The **eviction policy** is the rule that picks the victim.
This choice directly sets your **hit rate** — the fraction of requests served from
cache — which is the lever that determines whether the cache is worth having at all.
A policy that evicts the wrong entries turns a cache into expensive churn. The right
policy depends entirely on your *access pattern*: whether reuse is driven by recency,
frequency, or neither, and how much CPU and memory you are willing to spend tracking
that pattern.

## Mental model

Every eviction policy is a *bet about the future* made from the past. It predicts
which cached entry is **least likely to be requested again soon** and evicts that
one. The policies differ only in what signal they use to make the prediction:

- **Recency** (LRU): "what I touched recently, I'll touch again." Bets on temporal
  locality.
- **Frequency** (LFU): "what I touch often, I'll keep touching." Bets on a stable
  hot set.
- **Insertion order** (FIFO): "oldest in, first out," ignoring access entirely.
- **Nothing** (Random): no signal, just pick a victim cheaply.
- **Time** (TTL/TLRU): "truth expires," evict by age regardless of access.

Smarter policies (ARC, SLRU) combine recency *and* frequency and adapt the balance
online. More signal means higher hit rates but more bookkeeping per access.

## Types / Variants

**LRU (Least Recently Used).** Evicts the entry untouched for the longest. The
default for good reason: cheap (O(1) with a hash map + doubly-linked list), and most
real workloads have strong temporal locality. Weakness: a one-time scan over many
keys (a "scan" or "flush") evicts the genuinely hot set — it pollutes the cache.

**LFU (Least Frequently Used).** Evicts the entry with the fewest accesses. Keeps a
stable hot set resident and resists scan pollution. Weaknesses: needs counters
(more memory/CPU); a once-popular key with a high count can squat forever ("cache
pollution by stale popularity") unless counts decay. **Window-TinyLFU** (used by
Caffeine) fixes this with an aging sketch and is state of the art.

**FIFO (First In, First Out).** Evicts the oldest-inserted entry regardless of use.
Trivial to implement (a queue), but blind to access — it will happily evict a hot
key just because it was inserted long ago. Rarely optimal; common where simplicity
trumps hit rate. (Note: **CLOCK** is a low-cost LRU approximation often confused
with FIFO.)

**Random.** Evicts a uniformly random entry. O(1), no bookkeeping, no metadata.
Surprisingly competitive when access is uniform or when the working set far exceeds
cache size. Redis's `allkeys-random` and sampled approximations use this.

**TTL / TLRU (Time-aware).** Every entry carries an expiry; expired entries are
evicted (or treated as misses) regardless of access. **TLRU** = TTL combined with
LRU for the capacity dimension. Essential when staleness has a hard bound (you must
not serve data older than N seconds) — see *Cache invalidation*.

**ARC (Adaptive Replacement Cache).** Maintains two LRU lists — one for recently-seen
*once* (recency), one for seen *at least twice* (frequency) — plus ghost lists of
recently-evicted keys, and *adaptively* shifts capacity between recency and frequency
based on which is currently producing hits. High hit rates, self-tuning, no knobs.
Costs more metadata and was historically patent-encumbered (IBM); now widely
reimplemented.

**Segmented LRU (SLRU).** Splits the cache into a *probationary* segment (new
entries) and a *protected* segment (entries hit at least twice). A second hit
promotes an entry from probationary to protected; eviction takes from probationary
first. This gives scan resistance — a one-time scan never reaches the protected
segment. Used inside Caffeine and many CDN caches.

## When to use / When NOT

- **LRU — use when:** strong recency/temporal locality (most web and API workloads);
  you want a proven, cheap default. **Avoid when:** large sequential scans pollute
  it (analytics, full-table reads) — reach for SLRU/ARC instead.
- **LFU — use when:** a small, stable hot set dominates traffic (popular products,
  trending content) and you want it pinned. **Avoid when:** popularity shifts over
  time and your LFU has no decay/aging (use W-TinyLFU).
- **FIFO — use when:** simplicity is paramount and entries age out naturally; or as
  a building block. **Avoid when:** hit rate matters and access is non-uniform.
- **Random — use when:** access is near-uniform, metadata cost must be near zero, or
  you need a cheap approximation at huge scale. **Avoid when:** you have clear
  recency/frequency signal to exploit.
- **TTL/TLRU — use when:** staleness has a hard upper bound (auth tokens, prices,
  rate counters). **Avoid as your only policy** when memory pressure also needs a
  capacity-based victim — combine with LRU.
- **ARC / SLRU — use when:** mixed scan + hot-set workloads and you want maximum hit
  rate without manual tuning. **Avoid when:** you need dead-simple, dependency-free
  code or the extra metadata is too costly.

## Tradeoffs

| Policy | Signal | Hit rate (typical) | Per-access cost | Scan-resistant? |
| ------ | ------ | ------------------ | --------------- | --------------- |
| LRU | Recency | Good | O(1), low | No |
| LFU (W-TinyLFU) | Frequency | Very good | O(1), counters/sketch | Yes |
| FIFO | Insertion order | Poor–fair | O(1), minimal | No |
| Random | None | Fair | O(1), none | Partially |
| TTL / TLRU | Age (+ recency) | Workload-dependent | O(1) + expiry check | N/A |
| ARC | Recency + frequency (adaptive) | Excellent | O(1), more metadata | Yes |
| SLRU | Recency + reuse | Very good | O(1), two lists | Yes |

## Real-world examples

- **Redis** offers `noeviction`, `allkeys-lru`, `allkeys-lfu`, `allkeys-random`,
  `volatile-*` variants, and uses *sampled* (approximate) LRU/LFU to keep eviction
  O(1) at scale.
- **Caffeine** (the standard JVM cache) uses **Window-TinyLFU**, consistently at or
  near the top of academic hit-rate benchmarks.
- **CDNs** (Cloudflare, Fastly) use segmented/ARC-like policies with TTLs because
  edge traffic mixes a huge long tail with a hot head.
- **PostgreSQL** uses a CLOCK/LRU-approximation (clock-sweep) for its buffer pool;
  **MySQL InnoDB** uses a midpoint-insertion LRU specifically to resist scan
  pollution.
