## Overview

**Authorization (authz)** runs after authentication and answers a different
question: *given that we know who you are, what are you allowed to do?* It is the
control that decides whether Alice can read this invoice, edit that document, or
delete a tenant. Broken authorization is the **number-one** category in the OWASP
Top 10 because it fails quietly — the code works, the demo passes, and nobody
notices that user A can read user B's records by changing an ID in the URL. This
page covers the major authorization models — **RBAC, ABAC, ReBAC (Zanzibar),
ACLs** — where each fits, externalized **policy engines** like OPA, the
**least-privilege** and **scopes** disciplines, the hard problem of
**multi-tenant isolation**, and the **confused-deputy** trap where a privileged
service is tricked into acting on an attacker's behalf.

## Mental model

**Authorization is a function: `allow(subject, action, resource, context) -> bool`.**
Every model is just a different way of *organizing the data* that function reads
to reach a decision. The question that distinguishes them is **"where does the
permission live?"**

- In a **role** the subject holds (RBAC) — coarse, simple, role-shaped.
- In **attributes** of subject/resource/environment (ABAC) — fine-grained,
  rule-shaped.
- In **relationships** between subjects and resources (ReBAC) — graph-shaped,
  "Alice is an editor of Doc X because she is a member of Team Y that owns it."
- In a **list attached to each resource** (ACL) — resource-shaped.

The second durable idea: **decide deny-by-default and decide on the server.**
Authorization that the client can influence (a hidden button, a trusted request
field) is not authorization — it is a suggestion. Every access decision must be
enforced server-side, on every request, against the *real* identity, defaulting
to deny when no rule grants access.

## Types / Variants

**ACL (Access Control List).** Each resource carries an explicit list of
(subject → permission) entries: "file `report.pdf`: Alice=read, Bob=write." Dead
simple and precise per object, but it does not scale — managing millions of
resources × thousands of users individually is unmanageable, and "what can Alice
access?" becomes a full scan. Good for small, object-centric systems (filesystem
permissions, S3 object ACLs).

**RBAC (Role-Based Access Control).** Permissions are grouped into **roles**
(`admin`, `editor`, `viewer`); users are assigned roles; the role carries the
permissions. The dominant model because it matches how orgs think and is easy to
audit ("who are the admins?"). Weakness: **role explosion** — as you need
finer distinctions ("editor, but only for the EU region, only on weekdays"), you
either create a combinatorial blowup of roles or hit RBAC's expressiveness wall.

**ABAC (Attribute-Based Access Control).** Decisions are computed from
**attributes** of the subject (department, clearance), the resource (owner,
classification), the action, and the environment (time, IP, device posture),
evaluated by a policy rule: *"allow if `subject.dept == resource.dept` AND
`resource.classification <= subject.clearance`."* Extremely fine-grained and
context-aware, ideal for dynamic and compliance-driven rules — at the cost of
complexity: policies are harder to write, test, and reason about, and "why was
this allowed?" can be non-obvious.

**ReBAC (Relationship-Based Access Control) / Zanzibar.** Permissions derive from
a **graph of relationships** between subjects and resources. Access is computed
by traversing the graph: "Alice can edit Doc because Alice is a member of Team,
Team is an editor of Folder, Doc is in Folder." This is how Google models access
across its products via **Zanzibar**, a globally distributed authorization
system, and it is the right fit for **shared/nested resources and social-style
permissions** (Google Docs sharing, GitHub repo/org/team access). Open
implementations include **SpiceDB**, **Ory Keto**, and **OpenFGA**.

**Policy engines (externalized authz) — OPA.** Rather than scatter `if` checks
through application code, you **externalize** the decision to a dedicated engine.
**Open Policy Agent (OPA)**, with its **Rego** language, evaluates policies as a
separate component the app queries: "can subject X do action Y on resource Z?"
This decouples *policy* from *code*, lets security teams own and version policies
centrally, and gives uniform enforcement across services — at the cost of running
and reasoning about another moving part. (OPA can implement RBAC, ABAC, or
hybrids; ReBAC stores like SpiceDB are the graph-shaped counterpart.)

**Scopes (OAuth-style).** A coarse authorization grant attached to a **token**
that bounds what a *client/integration* may do on a user's behalf
(`read:repo`, `write:invoices`). Scopes constrain delegated access at the API
boundary; they are not a substitute for resource-level authorization inside the
app (a `read:invoices` scope still must not let you read *another tenant's*
invoices).

## When to use / When NOT

- **Use RBAC when:** permissions cluster naturally into a manageable set of job
  functions (most line-of-business apps). It's the right default. **Avoid /
  augment when:** you hit role explosion from per-region, per-time, or
  per-attribute conditions — add ABAC rules rather than minting 200 roles.
- **Use ABAC when:** decisions genuinely depend on dynamic context (time, device,
  data classification, regulatory geography). **Avoid when:** simple roles
  suffice — ABAC's flexibility is also debugging and audit pain you don't need.
- **Use ReBAC (Zanzibar-style) when:** resources are shared, nested, or
  hierarchical and permission flows through relationships (docs, folders, orgs,
  teams, social graphs). **Avoid when:** your model is flat and role-shaped —
  a relationship graph is overkill.
- **Use ACLs when:** a small, object-centric system where per-object lists are
  natural (filesystems, individual buckets). **Avoid at scale:** millions of
  resources make ACLs unmanageable and un-auditable.
- **Externalize to a policy engine (OPA) when:** you have many services needing
  consistent, centrally governed, auditable policy. **Avoid when:** a single app
  with simple rules — an extra component isn't worth it.
- **Apply least privilege and deny-by-default everywhere.** Grant the minimum;
  enforce server-side on every request; never trust client-supplied identity or
  permission hints.

## Tradeoffs

| Model | Granularity | Scales to | "What can X access?" | Best fit |
| ----- | ----------- | --------- | -------------------- | -------- |
| ACL | Per object | Small object sets | Hard (scan all objects) | Filesystems, single buckets |
| RBAC | Per role | Most orgs | Easy (list roles) | Line-of-business apps |
| ABAC | Per attribute/rule | Dynamic policies | Hard (evaluate rules) | Compliance, context-aware |
| ReBAC | Per relationship | Shared/nested resources | Graph traversal | Docs, orgs, social graphs |

The cross-cutting tension is **expressiveness vs auditability**: RBAC is easy to
audit but coarse; ABAC/ReBAC are precise but answering "who can touch this
resource, and why?" requires evaluating rules or traversing a graph. Most mature
systems end up **hybrid** — RBAC for the broad strokes, ABAC conditions or a
ReBAC graph for the fine ones — and externalize the result to a policy engine.

## Real-world examples

- **Google Zanzibar** is the planet-scale ReBAC system behind Docs, Drive,
  Calendar, and YouTube sharing; the paper inspired **SpiceDB**, **OpenFGA**, and
  **Ory Keto**.
- **AWS IAM** is a large-scale ABAC/policy system: JSON policies grant
  `(principal, action, resource, condition)`, and IAM roles plus
  least-privilege scoping are how the Capital One breach *should* have been
  contained.
- **Open Policy Agent (OPA)** is the CNCF-graduated policy engine widely used to
  externalize authz for Kubernetes admission control and microservice APIs via
  Rego policies.
- **The confused-deputy problem** — a privileged service tricked into misusing
  its authority on an attacker's behalf — is the core of **SSRF** (an internal
  service is made to fetch an attacker-chosen URL) and is mitigated by passing
  the *caller's* identity/scope, not the service's, into each downstream decision.
