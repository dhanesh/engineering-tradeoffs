## Overview

You cannot manage reliability you don't measure, and "is the site up?" is far too crude a
measure. The SRE discipline replaces gut feeling with three precise instruments: an **SLI**
(a metric of how well the service is doing — e.g. the fraction of requests served fast and
correctly), an **SLO** (the *target* for that SLI — e.g. "99.9% of requests succeed under
300ms"), and the **error budget** (the allowed shortfall — the `100% − SLO` of failures you
are *permitted*, and can spend deliberately). The **SLA** is the externally-promised,
contract-with-penalties version, usually looser than the internal SLO.

This page is about wielding those instruments as a *decision tool*, not a dashboard. The
error budget is the key move: it converts reliability from an absolute ("never go down")
into a *quantity you spend* — on releases, experiments, and risk. That reframing resolves
the oldest fight in operations: **developers want to ship fast, operators want to stay
stable.** The error budget makes the tradeoff explicit and self-policing — ship freely while
budget remains, slow down when it's exhausted. We cover choosing SLIs that actually reflect
user happiness, the **error-budget policy** that gives the number teeth, **multi-window
burn-rate alerting** (alert on *budget consumption rate*, not raw error rate), and **toil**
— the manual operational drag that erodes the time to do any of this.

## Mental model

**Reliability is a feature with a target, not a maximize-forever goal — and 100% is the
wrong target.** Two reasons it's wrong: (1) the marginal cost of each nine explodes, and (2)
your users can't even perceive reliability above the floor set by everything *between* you
and them (their wifi, their ISP, their phone). Chasing 100% spends unbounded money on
reliability the user will never notice — money better spent on features. So you pick a
*deliberately-imperfect* target (the SLO) that's just reliable enough, and the gap below 100%
is the **error budget** — a resource you get to *spend*.

That reframing is the whole point: **the error budget is a currency that aligns dev and
ops.** Every risky thing — a deploy, a migration, a new feature, a chaos experiment — spends
budget if it causes errors. While budget remains, the team ships at full speed; nobody can
block a release "to be safe." When the budget is **exhausted**, an agreed **policy**
automatically kicks in — freeze feature launches, redirect effort to reliability — until the
budget recovers. The number, not a person, makes the call. This dissolves the dev-vs-ops
political fight into an objective, pre-agreed rule.

Third model: **alert on the *rate you're burning the budget*, not on raw errors.** A static
"alert if error rate > 1%" is doomed — too sensitive and it pages on every blip (alert
fatigue), too lax and it misses a slow bleed that quietly drains the month's budget. The
**burn rate** — how fast you're consuming the error budget relative to "spend it evenly over
the window" — is the right signal. Burn rate `1` means you'll exactly exhaust the budget by
period end; burn rate `14.4` means you'll exhaust a 30-day budget in ~2 hours. **Multi-window,
multi-burn-rate** alerts page urgently on fast burns and ticket gently on slow burns —
high precision *and* high recall.

Fourth: **good SLIs measure the *user's* experience, not your server's internals.** CPU at
90% is not an SLI — users don't feel CPU. "99th-percentile request latency from the load
balancer" *is* — it's what users feel. Measure as close to the user as practical, and pick a
**threshold** (good/bad), not an average (averages hide the tail where the pain lives).

## Types / Variants

### The three instruments

- **SLI (Service Level Indicator).** A *measured* quantity of service quality, almost always
  a ratio of `good events / valid events` (e.g. `successful requests / total requests`,
  `requests under 300ms / total`). Common SLI classes: **availability** (success rate),
  **latency** (fraction under threshold), **throughput**, **correctness/quality**,
  **freshness** (for data pipelines), **durability**.
- **SLO (Service Level Objective).** The *target* for an SLI over a window — "99.9% of HTTP
  requests succeed over a rolling 30 days." Internal, aspirational-but-binding. Usually
  stricter than the SLA so you have margin.
- **SLA (Service Level Agreement).** The *contractual* promise to customers, with penalties
  (refunds/credits) for breach. Deliberately looser than the SLO — you want to react to the
  SLO *before* you breach the SLA.
- **Error budget.** `100% − SLO`, expressed as allowed bad events over the window. A 99.9%
  SLO over 30 days = a budget of ~43 minutes of "down" (or 0.1% of requests failing). The
  amount of unreliability you're *allowed to spend*.

### Choosing good SLIs

- **User-centric** — measure what a user experiences (request success, page load), not
  internal resource use.
- **Measured close to the user** — at the load balancer / edge / client, not deep in a
  backend that can't see what the user actually got.
- **Threshold-based, not averages** — "% of requests under 300ms," not "mean latency."
  Averages mask the painful tail; percentiles (p95/p99) don't.
- **Few and meaningful** — a handful of SLIs that, if green, mean users are happy. Too many
  and none drive decisions.

### Error-budget policy (what gives it teeth)

The number is useless without a *pre-agreed* consequence:

- **Budget remaining → ship freely.** Launches, experiments, and risk are green-lit.
- **Budget exhausted → the policy triggers.** Typically: freeze non-critical feature
  launches, divert engineering to reliability work, require extra review, until the budget
  recovers. Agreed *in advance* by dev and ops leadership, so it's automatic, not a fight per
  incident.

### Burn-rate alerting

- **Burn rate** = budget consumed / budget that "should" be consumed by now. Rate `1` =
  on-pace to exactly exhaust by period end.
- **Multi-window, multi-burn-rate (the SRE workbook recommendation).** Pair a *fast* burn
  alert (e.g. burn rate ≥ 14.4 over 1 hour AND 5 min → page now; a sustained burn rate of
  14.4 would exhaust a 30-day budget in about 2 hours) with a *slow* burn alert (e.g. burn
  rate ≥ 1 over 6 hours → ticket). The short second window prevents alerting on an
  already-recovered spike.
  This gives high precision (few false pages) and high recall (catches slow bleeds).

### Toil

- **Toil** = manual, repetitive, automatable, reactive operational work that scales with
  service size and produces no lasting value (manually restarting a service, hand-running a
  failover). SRE caps toil (e.g. ≤50% of an engineer's time) and spends the rest *eliminating*
  it through automation — because toil crowds out the engineering that actually improves
  reliability.

## When to use / When NOT

- **Define SLOs when:** you have real users with expectations and you want to make
  reliability-vs-velocity decisions objectively (any production service that matters).
  **Don't over-SLO when:** an internal best-effort tool — a heavyweight SLO regime is
  overhead nobody acts on.
- **Use error budgets when:** dev and ops need a shared, depoliticized way to balance shipping
  against stability. **Avoid when:** leadership won't actually *honor* the policy — an error
  budget nobody enforces is a vanity metric, and instituting it without buy-in breeds cynicism.
- **Set the SLO from user expectations, not from current performance** — measure what users
  need, then engineer to it. **Don't set the SLO at 100%** — it's unachievable, unaffordable,
  and imperceptible above the user's own connection floor.
- **Use burn-rate alerts when:** you have an SLO and a budget to protect. **Avoid raw static
  error-rate alerts** as your primary signal — they trade precision against recall badly and
  cause alert fatigue.
- **Make the SLA looser than the SLO — always**, so you have a margin to react internally
  before you breach the customer contract. Setting SLA = SLO leaves no room to act.
- **Track and cap toil when:** ops load is growing with scale and engineers are drowning in
  manual work. The cap forces the automation investment that fixes the root cause.

## Tradeoffs

| Decision | Buys you | Costs / risk |
| -------- | -------- | ------------ |
| Stricter SLO (more nines) | Happier users, stronger SLA headroom | Exponential cost; less error budget → less room to ship |
| Looser SLO | More error budget → faster shipping | Risk of user-perceived unreliability; weaker SLA margin |
| Error-budget policy | Depoliticized dev/ops balance; self-policing | Worthless unless leadership enforces the freeze |
| Burn-rate (multi-window) alerts | High precision + recall; catches slow bleeds | More complex to configure than a static threshold |
| Static error-rate alerts | Dead simple | Alert fatigue or missed slow bleeds — bad precision/recall |
| Many SLIs | Fine-grained visibility | Noise; none drive decisions; maintenance burden |
| Capping toil (automate) | Frees engineering time; reduces human error | Upfront automation investment before payoff |

The master tradeoff is **reliability vs velocity**, and the error budget is the instrument
that *prices* it: reliability above the SLO is wasted spend, reliability below it is a debt
that auto-triggers a slowdown. Set the SLO where the *marginal* nine stops being worth more
than the features you'd otherwise ship.

SLIs derived from real user experience set the SLO, whose complement is the error budget.

budget); when it's exhausted the pre-agreed policy freezes launches and redirects effort to
reliability until the budget recovers — a self-correcting loop.

## Real-world examples

- **Google SRE** invented and operationalized SLIs/SLOs/error budgets, including the
  **error-budget policy** that freezes launches when the budget is spent and the
  **multi-window multi-burn-rate** alerting recipe — the entire vocabulary on this page is
  theirs.
- **Cloud provider SLAs** (e.g. compute at 99.99%, with service credits) are the contractual
  SLA layer; providers run *internal* SLOs stricter than the published SLA so they react
  before owing credits.
- **Prometheus + Grafana** with the **`slo-libsonnet` / Pyrra / Sloth** tooling implement
  SLO recording rules and multi-burn-rate alerts as code — the common open-source stack for
  putting this page into practice.
- **Datadog, Nobl9, and Honeycomb** ship managed SLO products with burn-rate alerting,
  reflecting how mainstream the discipline has become beyond Google.
- **Fintech/payments platforms** run tight SLOs (often four-plus nines on the payment path)
  with strict error-budget policies, because the budget on a money-moving path is small and a
  burn directly maps to failed transactions and regulatory scrutiny.
