## Overview

A single instance of anything has a single point of failure: when it dies, the service
dies with it. **Redundancy** is the answer — run more than one of every critical
component so the loss of any one is survivable — and **failover** is the act of shifting
work from a failed component to a healthy one. Together they are how availability is
actually *bought*: the nines in [the overview](/reliability/index/) come from removing
single points of failure and recovering fast when a component is lost.

This page covers the engineering choices inside that idea: **active-active vs
active-passive** topologies, the redundancy math (**N+1, N+2, 2N**), the mechanics of
**health checks → failover → failback**, the physical isolation hierarchy
(**multi-AZ, multi-region**), and the two numbers that quantify a disaster-recovery
posture (**RTO and RPO**). It closes on a distinction teams routinely get wrong: **backups
and replication are not substitutes** — one protects against *data loss / corruption*,
the other against *component loss*, and you need both.

## Mental model

**Redundancy converts a single point of failure into a recoverable event — but only if
failover is automatic, tested, and fast.** Redundancy *without* working failover is
theater: a standby that nobody has verified will take over is, in the moment of failure,
just expensive idle hardware. The discipline is three linked questions:

1. **How many spares?** — N+1 (one spare), N+2 (survive a spare failing *during* a
   failure), or 2N (a full duplicate). More spares = more cost, more survivable.
2. **Are the spares working or waiting?** — **active-active** (all serve traffic, you
   lose capacity on failure but failover is instant) vs **active-passive** (standby waits
   idle, simpler but you pay for idle capacity and failover has a delay).
3. **How fast and how lossless is the cutover?** — quantified by **RTO** (how long to
   recover) and **RPO** (how much data you can lose). These two numbers, set by the
   *business*, dictate the entire architecture.

The second model: **the loss you're protecting against has a hierarchy, and your
redundancy must match it.** A redundant process survives a crash; redundant hosts survive
a machine; **multiple availability zones** survive a datacenter/power/network failure;
**multiple regions** survive a whole-region outage or natural disaster. Each level up is
more expensive and adds latency/consistency complexity — so you buy only the level your
RTO/RPO and threat model demand.

The third, often-missed model: **replication protects availability; backups protect
durability — they are orthogonal.** Replication keeps a *live, current* copy so you can
fail over instantly — but it faithfully replicates *corruption and bad deletes too*. A
backup is a *point-in-time, offline* copy that lets you recover from a logical disaster
(someone dropped the table, ransomware, a bad migration) that replication would have
dutifully propagated to every replica. **"We have replicas" is not a backup strategy.**

## Types / Variants

### Topologies

- **Active-passive (failover).** One node serves; one or more standbys wait, kept warm by
  replication. On failure, promote a standby. **Pro:** simple, no write-conflict problem
  (one writer). **Con:** idle capacity you pay for; a failover *gap* (detection +
  promotion time) during which you're down; standby may be stale (RPO > 0). Standby can be
  *hot* (ready instantly), *warm* (needs brief spin-up), or *cold* (provisioned on
  demand — cheapest, slowest).
- **Active-active.** All nodes serve traffic simultaneously behind a load balancer. **Pro:**
  no idle capacity, near-zero failover time (just stop routing to the dead node), and you
  get horizontal scale for free. **Con:** multi-writer complexity — conflict resolution,
  consistency, and you must run with enough headroom that losing one node doesn't overload
  the rest.

### Redundancy levels

- **N+1.** Provision one spare beyond the N needed for load. Survives one failure. The
  common, cost-effective default.
- **N+2.** Two spares — survives a second failure *while the first is still being
  repaired* (or during planned maintenance). For higher-stakes systems.
- **2N (full redundancy).** A complete duplicate of capacity — survives losing half of
  everything. Expensive; used where a whole site/path must be survivable.
- **2N+1.** Full duplicate *plus* a spare — belt and suspenders for critical
  infrastructure (power, networking).

### Failover mechanics

- **Health checks.** The trigger. *Shallow* checks ("is the port open / does `/healthz`
  return 200?") catch crashes but miss **gray failures**; *deep* checks exercise real
  dependencies (can it reach the DB?) and catch partial failure. Watch for **flapping** —
  a node bouncing healthy/unhealthy — and damp it with thresholds (N consecutive failures).
- **Failover.** Detect the failure, then redirect traffic — by promoting a replica,
  updating DNS, reassigning a virtual IP, or simply de-listing the node from the load
  balancer. **Leader election** picks the new primary in clustered systems (see
  [Coordination](/distributed-systems/coordination/)).
- **Failback.** Returning to the original (or repaired) node once it's healthy. Often the
  *riskier* operation — a hasty failback to a node that hasn't fully caught up can lose
  data or cause a second outage. Many shops avoid automatic failback entirely and treat
  the new primary as primary.
- **Split-brain.** The danger when failover misfires: two nodes both believe they're
  primary (typically during a network partition) and both accept writes, diverging the
  data. Prevented by **quorum / fencing** — only the side with majority may act (see
  [Distributed failure](/distributed-systems/distributed-failure/)).

### Isolation hierarchy (what you survive)

- **Multi-AZ** — replicas in separate availability zones (independent power/cooling/network
  in one region). Survives a datacenter-level failure with low inter-AZ latency. The
  baseline for serious production.
- **Multi-region** — replicas in geographically distant regions. Survives a whole-region
  outage or regional disaster, and serves users with lower latency — at the cost of
  cross-region replication lag (so RPO > 0 for async) and far more consistency complexity.

## When to use / When NOT

- **Active-passive — use when:** you need a single writer for correctness (most relational
  primaries), simplicity matters, and a few seconds/minutes of failover gap is acceptable.
  **Avoid when:** you need near-zero RTO or want to use the standby capacity — idle standbys
  are wasted money.
- **Active-active — use when:** you need near-zero failover time, want to use all capacity,
  and can handle multi-writer concurrency (stateless tiers; or stores built for it —
  Cassandra, DynamoDB global tables). **Avoid when:** a single writer is required for
  correctness and conflict resolution would be error-prone (e.g. a strongly-consistent
  ledger primary).
- **Multi-AZ — use when:** you run anything production-serious; it's the modern baseline and
  cloud-native. **Multi-region — add when:** your RTO/RPO requires surviving a full region,
  you serve a global audience needing low latency, or regulation mandates geographic DR.
  **Don't pay for multi-region** when multi-AZ already meets your RTO/RPO — it roughly
  doubles cost and complexity.
- **N+1 — use when:** single-failure tolerance is enough (most systems). **N+2 / 2N — use
  when:** you must tolerate a failure *during* maintenance or a second concurrent failure,
  or for foundational infra (power, network) where loss is catastrophic.
- **Backups — always**, in addition to replication, when data is valuable. **Never rely on
  replication as your backup** — it propagates corruption and deletes. Test restores, not
  just backups: an untested backup is Schrödinger's data.

## Tradeoffs

| Choice | Buys you | Costs / risk |
| ------ | -------- | ------------ |
| Active-passive | Single writer, simple, no conflicts | Idle standby cost; failover gap (RTO > 0); possible stale standby |
| Active-active | Near-zero RTO, full capacity use, scale | Multi-writer conflicts; need headroom for node loss; consistency complexity |
| N+1 | Cheap single-failure tolerance | Doesn't survive a second concurrent failure |
| 2N | Survives losing half | ~2x infrastructure cost |
| Multi-AZ | Survives a datacenter; low latency | Modest cross-AZ cost; still one-region risk |
| Multi-region | Survives a region; global low latency | Replication lag (RPO), big cost + consistency complexity |
| Sync replication | RPO ≈ 0 (no data loss) | Higher write latency; a slow replica stalls the primary |
| Async replication | Fast writes, decoupled | RPO > 0 — recent writes can be lost on failover |
| Replication | Live copy, instant failover | Propagates corruption/deletes — not a backup |
| Backups | Recover from corruption/disaster | Point-in-time (RPO = backup interval); restore takes time |

The master tradeoffs: **RTO trades against cost** (faster recovery needs hotter, more
redundant standby), and **RPO trades against write latency** (zero data loss needs
synchronous replication, which slows every write). The business sets RTO/RPO; the
architecture follows.

The health checker promotes a standby only after a *threshold* of consecutive deep-check
failures (to avoid flapping on a transient blip). The dangerous case is a network
partition where the old primary is still alive and reachable by some clients — fencing or
a quorum requirement ensures only the side holding majority may accept writes, so the two
sides never diverge.

## Real-world examples

- **AWS RDS Multi-AZ** is textbook **active-passive**: a synchronous standby in another AZ,
  with automatic failover on primary failure (RTO typically 1–2 minutes) — and AWS is
  explicit that the read-replica/standby is *not* a backup; you still need snapshots.
- **DynamoDB global tables** and **Cassandra** are **multi-region active-active**: every
  region accepts writes, with last-writer-wins / conflict resolution — the model when
  near-zero RTO across regions matters more than single-writer simplicity.
- **Kubernetes** runs **N+1/N+2** via replica counts and reschedules pods off a failed
  node; **liveness/readiness probes** are its shallow/deep health checks, and it de-routes
  unready pods automatically.
- **Datacenter power (2N / 2N+1)** — redundant UPS and generator paths so the facility
  survives a power-path failure during maintenance; the canonical use of full redundancy.
- **The classic "replica isn't a backup" outages** — GitLab's 2017 incident (a replica
  was the only "backup," and several backup methods were silently broken) is the industry's
  standing lesson that **untested backups + replication-as-backup = data loss**.
