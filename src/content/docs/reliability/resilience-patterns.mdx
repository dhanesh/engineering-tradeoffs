## Overview

When a dependency is slow, failing, or overloaded, the *caller* decides whether a blip
becomes an outage. The resilience patterns on this page are the caller-side toolkit:
the small set of well-understood mechanisms — **timeouts, retries, circuit breakers,
bulkheads, fallbacks, load shedding, hedging, idempotency** — that keep one bad
dependency from cascading across your system. The theory of *why* failures amplify
(retry storms, cascading and metastable failure) is in
[Distributed failure](/distributed-systems/distributed-failure/); this page is the
engineering catalog of *what to apply and when*.

The patterns compose, and the order matters. A correct resilient call stack is roughly:
**bound the call (timeout) → isolate it (bulkhead) → retry it safely (bounded, jittered,
idempotent) → stop hammering a dead dependency (circuit breaker) → have a plan B
(fallback / graceful degradation) → and protect *yourself* from overload (load shedding,
rate limiting)**. Get any layer wrong — especially unbounded retries or a missing
timeout — and the other layers can't save you.

## Mental model

**Every remote call is a liability you must bound on three axes: time, concurrency, and
blast radius.** Most outages trace to an *unbounded* one of these.

- **Time** — a call with no timeout can hang forever, and a hung call holds a thread,
  a connection, and memory. Threads pile up behind one slow dependency until the whole
  service is exhausted. **The timeout is the most important resilience pattern; a
  missing timeout is the most common cause of cascading failure.**
- **Concurrency** — without a bound on *how many* concurrent calls a dependency can
  consume, one slow dependency drains the shared pool and starves *every other*
  dependency. The **bulkhead** caps that: a dedicated, bounded pool per dependency, so
  one sinking compartment doesn't flood the ship.
- **Blast radius / load** — retries multiply load exactly when a dependency is least
  able to take it. The **circuit breaker** detects sustained failure and *stops calling*
  — failing fast and giving the dependency room to recover instead of pouring fuel on
  the fire.

The second model: **retries are a loaded gun.** They're mandatory for transient faults
but they *amplify* load, and naïvely they synchronize into storms. Safe retrying needs
four guards together — **(1) only on idempotent operations, (2) bounded attempts,
(3) exponential backoff with jitter, and (4) a retry budget** that caps retries as a
*fraction* of total traffic (e.g. retries ≤ 10% of requests) so a broad failure can't
triple your load. A retry without all four is a future incident.

The third model: **degrade, don't disappear.** When a dependency is gone, the resilient
move is rarely "return an error" — it's **fallback**: serve stale cache, a default, a
reduced feature, or a queued-for-later acknowledgement. The user gets *something*. This
is **graceful degradation**: the system sheds capability, not availability.

## Types / Variants

### Bounding and isolation

- **Timeout.** Cap how long you wait. Set it from the dependency's latency distribution
  (e.g. just above p99), not a round number. **Always finite.** Distinguish *connect*,
  *read/request*, and *overall* timeouts. A deadline that propagates down the call chain
  (deadline propagation) is the mature form — each hop subtracts elapsed time.
- **Bulkhead.** Isolate resources (thread pools, connection pools, semaphores) per
  dependency so one saturated dependency can't consume the pool the others need. Named
  for a ship's watertight compartments. Limits the blast radius of a single dependency's
  slowdown to that compartment.

### Retry and its guards

- **Retry.** Re-issue a failed call. Only for *transient* faults (timeouts, 5xx,
  connection resets) and only on *idempotent* operations or with an idempotency key.
- **Bounded attempts.** Hard cap (e.g. 3). Unbounded retry loops are how a blip becomes
  a metastable outage.
- **Exponential backoff.** Wait `base · 2^attempt`, capped, so you don't hammer a
  struggling dependency.
- **Jitter.** Randomize the backoff so thousands of clients that failed at the same
  instant don't retry in synchronized waves. **Full jitter** (`random(0, min(cap,
  base·2^n))`) is the AWS-recommended default.
- **Retry budget.** Cap retries as a percentage of total requests (e.g. 10%). Unlike
  per-call caps, the budget bounds *aggregate* retry load — the thing that actually
  causes retry storms. Used by Envoy/gRPC.

### Stopping the bleed

- **Circuit breaker.** A state machine that monitors failure rate to a dependency and,
  once it crosses a threshold, **opens** — short-circuiting calls to fail *fast* instead
  of waiting on a known-bad dependency. After a cooldown it goes **half-open** to probe,
  and **closes** again on success. The single most important cascade-stopper.
- **Load shedding.** Under overload, *reject* low-priority work early (429/503) rather
  than accept everything and collapse. Keeps the system in a recoverable regime. Prefer
  shedding cheap-to-reject work and protecting high-value requests.
- **Rate limiting / throttling.** Bound the request rate a client or tenant can consume
  so one noisy neighbor can't starve others. (Full treatment in
  [Rate limiting & throttling](/scalability/rate-limiting-throttling/).)

### Latency and correctness patterns

- **Hedged requests.** For latency-sensitive reads, send a *second* request to another
  replica if the first hasn't answered by some threshold (e.g. p95), and take whichever
  returns first. Cuts tail latency dramatically. Costs extra load, so cap hedging (e.g.
  only the slowest 5%) and require idempotency. (From Dean & Barroso, "The Tail at
  Scale.")
- **Fallback / graceful degradation.** A defined plan B when the call fails: stale
  cache, default value, reduced feature, or queue-for-later. Turns a failure into a
  degraded-but-useful response.
- **Idempotency.** Make repeated execution have the same effect as one execution
  (idempotency key + server-side dedup). The *precondition* that makes retries and
  hedging safe — without it, both duplicate side effects (double charges). The bridge to
  "effectively-once" (see [Failure handling](/reliability/failure-handling/)).

## When to use / When NOT

- **Timeout — always.** Every network call gets a finite timeout derived from the
  dependency's latency profile. **Never** issue a remote call with no timeout — there is
  no scenario where infinite waiting is correct.
- **Retry — use when:** the fault is transient *and* the operation is idempotent.
  **Avoid when:** the error is permanent (4xx/validation — retrying wastes capacity), or
  the op is non-idempotent without an idempotency key (you'll duplicate effects).
- **Circuit breaker — use when:** a dependency can fail or hang and you'd otherwise pile
  up retries against it (i.e. essentially every external dependency at scale). **Avoid /
  tune carefully when:** call volume is very low (statistically noisy — the breaker
  trips on a few flukes) or the call has no alternative and failing fast helps no one.
- **Bulkhead — use when:** multiple dependencies share a resource pool and one's
  slowdown would starve the others. **Skip when:** there's effectively one dependency and
  the isolation overhead buys nothing.
- **Load shedding — use when:** you can be overloaded (always, at scale) and would rather
  serve some traffic well than all traffic badly. **Avoid shedding** high-value/already-
  paid-for work first — shed cheap, low-priority traffic.
- **Hedged requests — use when:** reads are idempotent, replicas exist, and tail latency
  matters. **Avoid when:** requests are writes/non-idempotent, or the extra load can't be
  afforded — hedging *adds* load.
- **Fallback — use when:** a degraded answer beats an error (most read paths: stale data
  is usually better than no data). **Avoid stale fallbacks when:** correctness is
  absolute (showing a stale account balance can be worse than an honest error).

## Tradeoffs

| Pattern | Buys you | Costs / risk |
| ------- | -------- | ------------ |
| Timeout | Bounds hung calls; frees resources | Too tight → false failures on slow-but-ok calls; too loose → slow detection |
| Retry (bounded + backoff + jitter) | Recovers transient faults | Amplifies load; duplicates effects if non-idempotent; latency added |
| Retry budget | Caps *aggregate* retry load → kills storms | One more knob; needs central enforcement (mesh/SDK) |
| Circuit breaker | Fails fast; stops cascades; lets dependency recover | False trips shed good traffic; noisy at low volume; tuning required |
| Bulkhead | Isolates one dependency's slowdown | Lower utilization (reserved pools); more pools to size |
| Load shedding | Stays recoverable under overload | Rejects some requests (partial unavailability) |
| Hedged requests | Slashes tail latency | Extra load; requires idempotency + replicas |
| Fallback / degradation | Useful answer instead of an error | Stale/partial data; can mask real failures if overused |
| Idempotency | Makes retry + hedging safe | Dedup store + key management overhead |

The master discipline: **bound everything (time, concurrency, retries), make effects
idempotent, and degrade rather than disappear.** The single highest-leverage pattern is
the humble timeout; the single most dangerous omission is an unbounded retry.

## Diagram

The circuit breaker state machine — the heart of cascade prevention. It rides three
states: **closed** (calls flow, failures counted), **open** (calls short-circuit and
fail fast), and **half-open** (a few trial calls probe recovery).

**Closed** is normal operation — calls pass and failures accumulate in a rolling window.
Crossing the failure threshold trips to **open**, where every call fails fast (returning
a fallback) without touching the sick dependency — this is what stops the retry storm and
lets the dependency recover. After a cooldown the breaker goes **half-open** and admits a
*small* number of probe requests; if they succeed it **closes** (full traffic resumes),
if any fails it snaps back to **open** and restarts the timer. The half-open gate is the
crucial detail: it prevents slamming a just-recovering service with full traffic the
instant the cooldown ends.

## Real-world examples

- **Netflix Hystrix** (and its successor **resilience4j**) popularized the
  **circuit breaker + bulkhead + timeout + fallback** combination as a standard library,
  making these patterns mainstream in the JVM microservice world.
- **Envoy / Istio service mesh** implements **retries with budgets, timeouts, circuit
  breaking (outlier detection), and load shedding** at the proxy layer — so resilience is
  enforced uniformly without touching application code.
- **gRPC** ships built-in **retry policies with backoff + a retry throttle (budget)** and
  **deadline propagation**, the modern reference implementation of safe retrying.
- **AWS SDKs** implement **exponential backoff with full jitter** by default after AWS
  published the canonical analysis showing full jitter beats plain backoff at defusing
  retry storms.
- **Google (Dean & Barroso, "The Tail at Scale")** documented **hedged and tied
  requests** as the production technique for taming tail latency in large fan-out
  services like web search.
- **Stripe** requires **idempotency keys** on write requests so a client retry after a
  timeout can't double-charge — the precondition that makes the whole retry machinery safe
  for money (directly relevant to fintech ledgers).
