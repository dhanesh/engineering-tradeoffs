## Overview

Every other page in this cluster reduces how *often* and how *badly* systems fail; this page
is about the human-and-process machinery for the failures that happen anyway — **detecting**
them fast, **responding** to them under pressure without making things worse, and **learning**
from them so the same outage doesn't recur. Incident management is where reliability stops
being an architecture diagram and becomes an organizational capability: the on-call rotation,
the runbook, the incident commander, the severity ladder, and — most importantly — the
**blameless postmortem** that turns each outage into a permanent improvement.

The through-line is the **resilience flywheel**: every incident is a free (if painful) lesson,
and a mature organization extracts that lesson and feeds it back — into better detection,
better runbooks, better architecture, and proactively-discovered weaknesses via **chaos
engineering** and **Game Days**. Done well, the system gets *more* reliable with every failure;
done badly (blame, no follow-up), the same incidents recur and the team burns out. This page is
the operational complement to the measurement discipline in
[SLOs & error budgets](/reliability/slo-sli-error-budgets/): SLOs tell you *when* reliability is
slipping; incident management is *what you do about it*.

## Mental model

**An incident's total cost is `MTTD + MTTR`, and most of it is usually detection and
coordination, not the fix.** The clock starts when the system breaks, not when you notice. So
the biggest wins are often *not* "fix faster" but **"detect faster" (MTTD)** and **"coordinate
better."** A bug you find in 30 seconds via good alerting and fix in 2 minutes beats one you
discover from angry customers an hour later. The phases — **detect → triage → mitigate → resolve
→ learn** — each have their own discipline, and **mitigation comes before resolution**: stop the
bleeding (roll back, fail over, shed load) *first*, find and fix the root cause *after*. Trying
to root-cause a live outage before mitigating is the classic rookie error.

The second model: **under incident pressure, structure beats heroics.** A major incident with
five engineers all debugging in parallel, nobody coordinating, and stakeholders interrupting for
updates is *slower*, not faster. The **Incident Command System** imposes roles — an **Incident
Commander** (coordinates, decides, owns the incident — does *not* debug), **Operations/responders**
(do the hands-on work), and a **Communications lead** (updates stakeholders) — so the responders
can focus while one person holds the big picture. Clear roles turn chaos into a controlled
response. **Severity levels** (SEV1–SEV4) then calibrate the response: a SEV1 (full outage,
revenue-impacting) pages leadership and convenes incident command; a SEV4 is a ticket.

The third, and culturally most important model: **postmortems must be blameless, because the
goal is to fix the system, not the human.** People don't cause outages; *systems that allow a
human action to cause an outage* cause outages. If postmortems assign blame, people hide
mistakes, information dries up, and you lose the learning — the opposite of what you need. A
blameless postmortem assumes everyone acted reasonably with the information they had, and asks
"what about the *system* let this happen, and how do we make it impossible (or harmless) next
time?" Every postmortem produces **tracked action items** with owners — a postmortem with no
follow-through is theater.

The fourth model: **don't wait for failures — manufacture them on purpose.** **Chaos
engineering** injects controlled failures (kill a node, add latency, sever a dependency) into
*production or near-production* to discover weaknesses *before* they discover you — turning
unknown failure modes into known, fixed ones. **Game Days / DiRT** (Disaster Recovery Testing)
are the scheduled, organization-level version: rehearse a major outage, exercise the runbooks
and the humans, and find the gaps when the stakes are a drill, not a real incident. You only know
your failover works if you've actually triggered it.

## Types / Variants

### Detection

- **MTTD (Mean Time To Detect).** The front-half of every incident; minimizing it is
  high-leverage. Driven by good alerting — ideally **symptom-based** (SLO burn rate, user-facing
  error/latency), not just **cause-based** (a single host's CPU). Symptom alerts catch gray
  failures that cause-based alerts miss.
- **Alert quality.** Every alert must be *actionable* and *urgent*. Non-actionable or noisy
  alerts cause **alert fatigue** — responders tune out, and the real page gets missed. Page on
  what needs a *human now*; ticket the rest.

### Response roles & structure

- **On-call.** A rotation of engineers responsible for responding to pages. Sustainable on-call
  (reasonable rotation length, follow-the-sun for global teams, compensation, a cap on pages per
  shift) is a reliability *and* humane-ops concern — a burned-out on-call is a slow on-call.
- **Runbooks.** Documented, step-by-step procedures for known failure modes ("if the queue
  backs up, do X"). They turn tribal knowledge into a checklist anyone on-call can execute at
  3am, cutting MTTR and reducing error under stress. Best when linked directly from the alert.
- **Incident Command System (ICS).** Roles for major incidents: **Incident Commander** (owns
  coordination and decisions, does not debug), **Ops lead / responders** (hands-on fix),
  **Comms lead** (stakeholder updates), **Scribe** (timeline). Borrowed from emergency services;
  scales the response without chaos.
- **Severity levels (SEV1–SEV4).** Calibrate response to impact. SEV1 = critical/full outage,
  all-hands + leadership; SEV2 = major degradation; SEV3 = minor/partial; SEV4 = low/cosmetic.
  The severity drives who's paged, how often you update, and whether you convene incident command.

### Learning

- **Blameless postmortem.** A written, no-blame analysis after a significant incident: timeline,
  impact, root cause(s), what went well/badly, and **tracked action items with owners**. The
  mechanism that converts an outage into durable improvement.
- **The "5 Whys" / contributing factors.** Push past the proximate cause to the systemic ones —
  but reject single-root-cause thinking; complex outages have *multiple* contributing factors, and
  fixing only the last one leaves the others armed.
- **Action-item tracking.** Postmortem follow-ups are treated like any prioritized work and
  closed out — otherwise the same incident recurs and trust in the process erodes.

### Proactive resilience

- **Chaos engineering.** Deliberately inject failures (instance kill, latency, dependency loss,
  zone outage) under controlled conditions to surface weaknesses before they cause real incidents.
  Start in staging, graduate to production with a small blast radius and an abort switch.
- **Game Days / DiRT.** Scheduled disaster rehearsals at team/org scale — exercise runbooks,
  on-call, and failover end-to-end so the humans and the procedures are tested, not just the code.
- **The resilience flywheel.** Each incident → postmortem → action items → improved detection,
  runbooks, and architecture → fewer/shorter incidents → capacity to invest in proactive chaos →
  even fewer incidents. The compounding loop that makes a system reliable *over time*.

## When to use / When NOT

- **Invest in MTTD (symptom-based alerting) when:** you have users who notice outages before you
  do (the painful common case). **Avoid cause-based-only alerting** — it misses gray failures and
  pages on noise. **Don't over-alert** — every non-actionable page erodes the next real one.
- **Use the Incident Command System when:** an incident is large enough that uncoordinated
  responders would collide (SEV1/SEV2). **Skip the formality when:** it's a routine SEV3/SEV4 one
  person can handle — ICS overhead on a small incident is bureaucracy.
- **Write runbooks for:** known, recurring, or high-stakes failure modes, linked from the alert.
  **Don't try to runbook everything** — novel incidents need judgment, not a script; runbooks
  cover the *known* failures and free attention for the unknown.
- **Run blameless postmortems for:** every significant incident (SEV1/SEV2, customer impact, or a
  near-miss worth learning from). **Never run a blameful one** — blame destroys the information
  flow that makes postmortems valuable. **Don't skip the action-item follow-through** — that's the
  whole point.
- **Do chaos engineering when:** the system is mature enough that you have monitoring to *observe*
  the injected failure and a way to abort, and the cost of an undiscovered failure mode is high.
  **Avoid chaos in production when:** you lack the observability or rollback to do it safely — you'll
  just cause the outage you were trying to prevent. Start in staging.
- **Hold Game Days / DiRT when:** you have DR procedures that have *never been exercised* (untested
  failover is Schrödinger's failover). **Always rehearse before you need it** — the incident is the
  wrong time to discover the runbook is wrong.

## Tradeoffs

| Practice | Buys you | Costs / risk |
| -------- | -------- | ------------ |
| Symptom-based alerting | Catches real user pain + gray failures; lower MTTD | Needs SLOs/good telemetry; tuning to avoid noise |
| Aggressive alerting | Catch everything fast | Alert fatigue → the real page gets ignored |
| Runbooks | Fast, low-error response under stress | Maintenance burden; staleness if not exercised |
| Incident Command System | Coordinated response; responders stay focused | Overhead/ceremony on small incidents |
| Severity levels | Right-sized response per impact | Mis-grading wastes effort or under-responds |
| Blameless postmortems | Durable learning; honest information flow | Useless without action-item follow-through; needs real culture buy-in |
| Chaos engineering | Finds failure modes proactively, on your terms | Can cause real incidents without safeguards; investment |
| Game Days / DiRT | Tested runbooks, people, and failover | Costs engineering time; disruptive to schedule |

The master discipline: **detect on symptoms, mitigate before you root-cause, coordinate with
roles, learn blamelessly with tracked follow-up, and manufacture failures proactively.** The
master mindset is the **resilience flywheel** — treat every incident as fuel for a system that
gets more reliable over time, rather than a one-off fire to forget.

The response path runs detect → triage → **mitigate before resolve** (stop the bleeding before
hunting the root cause) → resolve. The learning path turns the incident into a blameless
postmortem with tracked action items, which feed improvements to detection, runbooks, and
architecture. Those improvements both reduce future incidents *and* enable proactive chaos
engineering and Game Days that surface weaknesses before they become incidents — the compounding
resilience flywheel.

## Real-world examples

- **Google SRE** codified the **Incident Command System for software** (commander / ops / comms
  roles), **blameless postmortems**, and **DiRT** (Disaster Recovery Testing) — the practices on
  this page are largely formalized from their published handbook and workbook.
- **PagerDuty / Opsgenie** operationalize **on-call rotations, escalation policies, and severity-
  driven paging**, and PagerDuty's open incident-response documentation is a widely-used template
  for ICS roles and postmortems.
- **Netflix Chaos Monkey / the Simian Army** pioneered **chaos engineering** — randomly killing
  production instances to force engineers to build services that tolerate it — which grew into the
  discipline and the *Principles of Chaos Engineering*.
- **AWS GameDay** and **Gremlin** turned chaos engineering and disaster rehearsal into managed
  products/events, making controlled fault injection accessible beyond Netflix-scale teams.
- **Etsy's "Blameless PostMortems and a Just Culture"** (John Allspaw) is the canonical industry
  essay on *why* blame destroys the learning loop — required reading that shaped how the industry
  runs postmortems.
