## Overview

This cluster is about the *discipline* of building systems that keep working when
their parts don't — the patterns, the measurements, and the operations practices.
The theory of *why* distributed systems fail (failure detectors, partitions, gray and
cascading failures, the impossibility of exactly-once) lives in
[Distributed failure](/distributed-systems/distributed-failure/); this cluster
assumes that theory and asks the engineering question instead: **given that
everything fails, how do you build, measure, and operate a system that survives it?**

That starts with precise vocabulary, because "reliability," "availability," and
"resilience" get used interchangeably and they are not the same thing — confusing
them leads teams to over-invest in the wrong place (chasing a fifth nine of
availability on a system whose real problem is a 4-hour recovery time). This page
sets the definitions, the math of the **nines** and what each one costs, the
**MTBF/MTTR** levers, the **fault → error → failure** chain that tells you where to
intervene, **blast radius** as the unit of damage control, and the central mindset:
**design for failure as the default**, not as an exception you bolt on later.

## Mental model

**Reliability is a property you measure, availability is a number you report, and
resilience is a behavior you engineer.** They relate but don't reduce to each other:

- **Reliability** — the probability the system does the *right* thing for a given
  period. It includes correctness, not just being up. A system returning wrong
  answers quickly is available but not reliable.
- **Availability** — the fraction of time the system is *usable*, the famous "nines."
  A purely uptime measure; it says nothing about correctness or how badly a failure
  hurt while it lasted.
- **Resilience** — the system's ability to *absorb* faults and *recover* — to degrade
  gracefully and bounce back rather than collapse. Resilience is *how* you achieve
  high reliability and availability in a world of constant faults.

The second durable model is the **fault → error → failure** chain, the language of
dependability:

- A **fault** is a defect — a bug, a dead disk, a bad config. It may lie dormant.
- An **error** is a fault *activated* — the system reaches a wrong internal state.
- A **failure** is an error that *escapes* — the service deviates from its spec as
  seen by a user.

You build reliability by breaking this chain at the cheapest point: prevent faults
(testing, review), tolerate errors so they don't become failures (redundancy,
retries, isolation), or contain failures so they don't spread (bulkheads, blast-radius
limits). **Most resilience engineering is error-containment** — you can't prevent
every fault, so you stop errors from propagating into user-visible failure.

Third: **`availability = MTBF / (MTBF + MTTR)`.** You raise availability two ways —
make failures *rarer* (higher Mean Time Between Failures) or make recovery *faster*
(lower Mean Time To Recovery). Past a point, chasing rarer failures gets
exponentially expensive, while **shrinking MTTR is usually the higher-leverage
investment**: a system that fails often but recovers in seconds can beat one that
fails rarely but takes hours. This is why the industry pivoted from "prevent all
failure" to "**recover fast**" — fast detection, fast rollback, fast failover.

Fourth: **blast radius** is the unit of damage control. When (not if) something
fails, *how much* breaks? One user, one shard, one AZ, one region, or everything?
Reliability engineering is largely the work of shrinking blast radius — cell-based
architecture, sharding, isolation — so a failure is a contained incident, not a
company-ending outage. **Design for failure as the default**: assume every
dependency will be down, slow, or wrong, and decide what your system does *then* —
before it happens, not during the incident.

## Types / Variants

### The dimensions of "doing the right thing"

Dependability is an umbrella over several distinct properties; teams that conflate
them mis-prioritize:

- **Availability** — usable when needed (uptime). Measured in nines.
- **Reliability** — correct over an interval (continuity of correct service).
- **Maintainability** — how fast you can repair/change it (drives MTTR).
- **Durability** — data survives (the "eleven nines" of object stores; distinct from
  service uptime — your data can be safe while the service is down).
- **Recoverability** — how completely and quickly you restore after a disaster
  (measured by RTO/RPO — see [Redundancy & failover](/reliability/redundancy-failover/)).

### The nines (and what each costs)

Availability is quoted as a percentage of uptime; each additional **nine** cuts
allowed downtime by `10x` and typically multiplies engineering cost:

- **Two nines (99%)** — ~3.65 days/year down. A single well-run server.
- **Three nines (99.9%)** — ~8.77 hours/year. Redundancy + monitoring + on-call.
- **Four nines (99.99%)** — ~52.6 min/year. Multi-AZ, automated failover, fast
  rollback, mature ops. Most SaaS SLAs top out here.
- **Five nines (99.999%)** — ~5.26 min/year. Multi-region, no manual steps in the
  recovery path — humans are too slow to hit five nines. Telecom/payments territory.
- **Six nines (99.9999%)** — ~31.5 sec/year. Exotic; usually aspirational marketing
  unless you're a core network/exchange.

The discontinuity: **each nine roughly multiplies cost**, and **the jump to five nines
removes humans from the loop** (5 minutes/year is less than one human reaction +
decision). Know which nine your *business* actually needs — most don't need five, and
buying it wastes money that fast-MTTR would spend better.

### The two levers: MTBF and MTTR

- **MTBF (Mean Time Between Failures)** — raise it by preventing faults: redundancy,
  better testing, hardened dependencies, capacity headroom. Diminishing returns and
  expensive at the tail.
- **MTTR (Mean Time To Recovery)** — lower it by recovering faster: good detection
  (MTTD), automated failover, instant rollback, runbooks, practiced incident response.
  Usually the cheaper, higher-leverage lever — and the one SRE optimizes.
- **MTTD (Mean Time To Detect)** is the hidden front-half of MTTR: you can't recover
  from what you haven't noticed. Most "long outages" are long *detection*, not long
  *fixes*.

### Blast-radius containment patterns

- **Sharding / partitioning** — a failure hits one shard's users, not all.
- **Cell-based architecture** — replicate the whole stack into independent cells;
  route each user to one cell; a cell failure caps the blast radius at `1/N` of users.
- **Availability zones / regions** — physical isolation so one datacenter's loss is
  survivable.
- **Bulkheads** — isolate resource pools so one overloaded dependency can't sink the
  whole process (see [Resilience patterns](/reliability/resilience-patterns/)).

## When to use / When NOT

- **Invest in higher availability (more nines) when:** downtime directly costs revenue
  or safety (payments, trading, health), or contractual SLAs demand it. **Don't chase
  nines when:** your users tolerate brief outages and the spend would buy more value
  as features or faster MTTR. Five nines on an internal analytics dashboard is waste.
- **Optimize MTTR over MTBF when:** failures are inevitable and recovery is currently
  slow (the common case) — fast rollback/failover beats marginally rarer failures.
  **Optimize MTBF when:** each failure is catastrophic or unrecoverable (data
  corruption, irreversible financial action) — there, prevention is non-negotiable.
- **Engineer for blast-radius containment when:** you have many tenants/users and a
  shared failure mode would take all of them down at once. **Skip heavy cell isolation
  when:** you're small enough that the operational complexity outweighs a still-rare,
  still-contained outage.
- **Design for failure as default — always.** There is no system at scale where
  "assume dependencies are healthy" is a safe default. The only question is whether you
  decide the failure behavior *now* or discover it *during the incident*.
- **Distinguish durability from availability when:** the data is irreplaceable
  (ledgers, customer records). You may accept the *service* being down briefly but never
  accept *losing the data* — different mechanisms (backups/replication vs failover).

## Tradeoffs

| Lever | Buys you | Costs / risk |
| ----- | -------- | ------------ |
| More nines of availability | Less downtime, stronger SLAs | Exponential cost per nine; complexity; over-investment if unneeded |
| Raise MTBF (prevent failures) | Failures happen less often | Diminishing returns; can't prevent everything; lulls you into slow recovery |
| Lower MTTR (recover faster) | Outages end fast; cheaper than prevention | Requires automation + practiced ops; needs investment in detection |
| Lower MTTD (detect faster) | Shrinks the front-half of every outage | Monitoring/alerting cost; alert fatigue if done badly |
| Shrink blast radius (cells/shards) | A failure is contained, not total | Routing complexity; per-cell overhead; harder cross-cell features |
| Redundancy (2N, multi-region) | Survives component/site loss | Multiplied infra cost; consistency + failover complexity |

The master tradeoff is **cost vs the marginal nine**, and the master insight is that
**MTTR and blast radius usually beat MTBF on cost-effectiveness**: build to recover
fast and fail small, rather than spending unboundedly to fail never.

failures that *do* escape into contained incidents instead of major outages.

## Real-world examples

- **AWS / Azure / GCP** publish per-service SLAs (commonly 99.9%–99.99%) with service
  credits as the penalty — the contractual face of "the nines," and a reminder that
  even hyperscalers don't promise five nines on most services.
- **AWS cell-based architecture** is the canonical **blast-radius** discipline:
  replicate the stack into independent cells so a failure caps at `1/N` of customers
  rather than all of them; documented in their Well-Architected and Builders' Library
  material.
- **S3's "eleven nines" of durability** (99.999999999%) shows **durability ≠
  availability**: your objects are essentially never lost even though the *service* has
  a far lower availability SLA — different property, different mechanism.
- **Google SRE** institutionalized **MTTR over MTBF**: error budgets, fast rollback,
  and practiced incident response over chasing zero failures — the explicit bet that
  recovering fast beats failing never.
- **Payment networks (Visa/UPI-scale)** operate near five nines because seconds of
  downtime cascade into financial and regulatory consequences — one of the few domains
  where the cost of the fifth nine is genuinely justified.
